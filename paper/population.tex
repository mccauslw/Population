\begin{abstract}
  We run an experiment covering 32 different choice domains, including fine art, cars and pizza.
  For each domain, there is a set of five related items, and 26 \menus{}---the subsets of two or more items.
  We measure population-level choice probabilities by presenting, within each domain, each of its 26 \menus{} to a distinct group of at least 40 participants (1042 participants in total) and observing each participant's choice of one option.
  Each participant faces exactly one \menu{} per domain.
  The purpose of the experiment is to allow tests of various conditions over choice probabilities, over a wide variety of choice domains.
  It is particularly well suited for testing conditions, like regularity, the random utility condition and the multiplicative inequality, which constrain choice probabilities across all \menus{} of a domain.
  For each domain and each of the three conditions above, we report Bayes factors giving evidence for or against the condition.
  Regularity and random utility, conditions that most discrete choice models satisfy, survive this battery of tests: across the 32 domains, Bayes factors generally support them and nowhere is there more than slight evidence against them.
  In contrast, very strong evidence against the multiplicative inequality in three domains shows that it cannot be considered as a universal condition on population-level choice.
  Classes of models implying the multiplicative inequality---including the classes of independent random utility models and Elimination by Aspects models---are not universally adequate for modelling population-level choice.
  \\
  Key words: Discrete choice, Stochastic choice, Choice experiment, Context effects, Random utility, Bayesian inference \\
  JEL codes: C11, C12, C15, C35, C91, D01, D90.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{s:intro}

Discrete choice models, which describe the choices of decision makers from finite menus of options, are used extensively in economics, psychology, marketing, transportation research, health research and other disciplines.
Most empirical applications seek to measure how attributes of choice options affect choice probabilities in particular cases; for example, how commute times and other features affect the probabilities of choosing various transportation options (e.g. \citeauthor{Trai78}, \citeyear{Trai78}).
Here, we contribute experimental data to a distinct and more abstract research agenda that collects evidence for or against, as the case may be, various testable conditions on choice probabilities, without regard to item attributes, across a wide variety of choice environments.

Consider the following choice experiment, one of 32 we performed using an internet survey.
We asked each participant ``Which one of the following cities do you think is furthest north?'' and then presented them with one {\em menu}, a list of at least two options taken from the following set of five cities: Warsaw, Poland; London, United Kingdom; Vancouver, Canada; Paris, France; and Seattle, United States.
The experimental manipulation consisted of varying the menu across participants.
There are 26 possible menus from the set of five cities and we presented each \menu{} to a distinct group of 40 or 41 participants (1042 participants in total).
Table \ref{t:latitude_table} shows the choice counts we observed, by menu and item.

The following examples illustrate several themes of the paper.
From the menu of Vancouver and Paris, 17 out of 40 participants chose Vancouver and 23 chose Paris; from the menu of Vancouver, Paris and Seattle, 26 out of 40 chose Vancouver; 14, Paris; and none, Seattle.
It appears that adding Seattle to the menu of Vancouver and Paris increases the probability that participants choose Vancouver.
This would be a violation of a condition on choice probabilities known as {\em regularity}, which holds that if you add an option to a menu, the probability of choosing an existing option cannot increase.

Regularity is one of many stochastic choice conditions that have been studied in the discrete choice literature.
It is easy to understand and has some intuitive appeal as a plausible axiom of choice.
We will see later that it is a condition that is implied by a large class of choice models.
Regularity can be regarded as a hypothesis and tested directly, allowing us to draw conclusions about this class of models without estimating model parameters.
It consists of a set of inequalities that jointly constrain choice probabilities across all 26 menus of the five-item universe described above.
Finally, regularity is an abstract condition: it makes no reference to attributes of various options and can therefore be applied across very different choice environments.

Regularity, and other conditions implied by various classes of models, are targets of a large literature on {\em context effects}.
This literature gathers empirical evidence against these conditions, and by extension, the models that imply them.
The attraction effect, for example, occurs when adding an option that is clearly inferior to one option in a menu increases the probability of choosing the superior option, a clear violation of regularity.
We designed the Latitude domain to include configurations of cities conducive to an attraction effect, based on our conjecture that it would be obvious to most participants, drawn from a panel of Canadian residents, that Vancouver is north of Seattle and would be seen as a clearly superior option.
Indeed, from the \menu{} consisting of these two cities, 36 chose Vancouver and 4 chose Seattle.
The choice counts reported above reveal that when Seattle is added to the menu of Vancouver and Paris, the proportion of participants choosing Vancouver increased.
This provides preliminary evidence for the attraction effect, but to assess the strength of the evidence, we must take into account sampling variation.

The main contribution of this paper is a publicly available dataset designed for testing abstract stochastic choice conditions, such as regularity, across a wide range of choice domains.
The experiment includes 32 diverse choice domains, including fine art, cars, and pizza; this variety allows us and others to discover properties of random choice behaviour that hold widely.
Some domains are specifically designed to be conducive to the attraction effect or other context effects.
We do so to stress-test conditions that are inconsistent with the effects and, more generally, to promote choice patterns that many consider anomalous.
Measuring choice probabilities on all menus allows us to test, jointly, the complete set of implications of a condition, such as regularity, that constrains choice probabilities over all menus.
In this way, we can expose every implication of the condition to empirical scrutiny.
Any unfavourable evidence is evidence against decision theories that imply the condition.

It documents three conditions on choice probabilities in detail, including regularity, and points to others in the literature.
It discusses the applicability of population-level data to inferences about individual-level behaviour.
It describes three context effects and outlines the motivation for designing some of the domains to favour them.

These methods are adapted from earlier work but are summarized here to make the paper reasonably self-contained and reproducible.

\section{Framework}\label{s:framework}

Here we set up the probabilistic choice framework by defining a {\em random choice model}.
We define three conditions on choice probabilities we investigate in a later empirical section: regularity, the Block-Marschak inequalities and the multiplicative inequality.
We describe versions of three context effects that motivated parts of our experimental design: the attraction, similarity and compromise effects.
Finally, we discuss individual- and population-level choice experiments and identify an aggregation property relating conditions on individual choice to conditions on population choice.

\subsection{Random choice models}

A random choice model specifies choice probabilities for a set $\univ = \{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\}$ of related choice items, which we call a universe.
For each of the 32 choice domains in our experiment, there is a  universe containing five related and easily comparable choice items.
We will write $\univ = \{a,b,c,d,e\}$ for a generic five-item universe.
For the ``Latitude'' domain of the experiment, where participants see the prompt question ``Which one of the following cities do you think is furthest north?'', $a$ is Warsaw, $b$ is London, $c$ is Vancouver, $d$ is Paris and $e$ is Seattle.

Each participant sees a single \menu{} from each domain and must choose a single option from each \menu{}.
Different participants see different \menus{}.
Since any \menu{} may be presented, a random choice model needs to specify choice probabilities for each option of each \menu{}.
For a given universe $\univ$, let $A$ be any non-empty subset of $\univ$ and $x$ be any option in $A$.
Define $P_A(x)$ as the probability of choosing $x$ from $A$, and $P_A$ as the vector of all choice probabilities on \menu{} $A$.
One of the 26 \menus{} is $\{a,b\}$; $P_{\{a,b\}}(a)$ and $P_{\{a,b\}}(b)$ are the probabilities of choosing Warsaw and London from the \menu{} consisting of these two cities and $P_{\{a,b\}}$ is the vector of both.
We learn something about these probabilities from the experiment: 22 participants chose Warsaw and 18 chose London, as we can see in the first row of Table \ref{t:latitude_table}, which tabulates choice counts for each of the 26 \menus{} of the ``Latitude'' domain.
We also define, for observed choice counts, $\hat P_A(x)$ as the proportion of times that an item $x$ is chosen from a \menu{} $A$, as long as $x$ is in $A$.
For example, we compute the observed choice proportions $\hat P_{\{a,b\}}(a) = 0.55$ and $\hat P_{\{a,b\}}(b) = 0.45$ for the ``Latitude'' domain.
Although we defined \menus{} earlier as choice sets with at least two items, the notation $P_A(x)$ allows for choice sets $A$ with a single item.
This is to ensure that we can concisely write down conditions like the Block-Marschak inequalities below; for each item $x$ in $\univ$, the choice probability $P_{\{x\}}(x)$ is trivially equal to one.

A random choice model for the universe $\univ$ is the complete specification of all choice probabilities $P_A(x)$ for all \menus{} $A$ of $U$ and all items $x$ in $A$, and is denoted $P$.
In the case of repeated trials, we assume that observed choices are statistically independent, both within and across menus.
We also assume that for each menu, observed choices are identically distributed.
Thus a random choice model $P$ for a universe gives a complete probabilistic description of an observed sequence of choices from menus of that universe.
In a manner analogous to the definition of $P$, we define $\hat P$ as the complete specification of all the choice proportions $\hat{P}_A(x)$ observed in an experiment.
By construction, $\hat P$ satisfies the same non-negativity and adding-up constraints that $P$ does.
The term random choice model is often used to describe logit and other, often parametric, sets of random choice models.
We will refer to these as {\em classes} of random choice models.

One particularly important class of random choice models consists of those that describe the behaviour of random utility maximizers.
Each time such a decision maker encounters a \menu{} in a choice trial, they draw random utility values for all items in the universe and then choose the option in that \menu{} with the highest utility.
Random utility is drawn independently of which \menu{} is being offered.
From trial to trial, draws are independent and identically distributed.
Within a trial, however, the utilities of the various items may be statistically dependent.
Henceforth, we refer to the condition defining this class of models simply as random utility.
Most classes of random choice models used in applied work satisfy the random utility condition, including the Logit and Probit classes.
For a detailed exposition of random utility and how it relates to random choice models, using the same notation as this paper, see \citet{McCaStobMarlParkBrow20}.

\subsection{Global testable conditions for stochastic choice}\label{s:conditions}

Our term ``condition'' covers any axiom, constraint, hypothesis, property, proposition or rule about probabilistic choice behaviour that can be expressed as a restriction over the various choice probabilities of a random choice model.

We have already seen regularity, the condition that if you add options to a \menu{}, the probability of choosing an existing option does not go up.
Formally, a random choice model $P$ for a universe $\univ$ of choice items satisfies regularity if and only if for all $x \in A \subseteq B \subseteq \univ$,
\[
  P_A(x) \geq P_B(x).
\]
\citet{LuceSupp65} describe regularity and show that it is necessary for the random utility condition.

Recall that regularity constrains choice probabilities across all \menus{}.
In fact, we can claim something stronger.
Every $P_A$ is {\em pivotal}, in the sense that for any random choice model satisfying regularity and any \menu{} $A$, one can change the vector $P_A$ so that the modified model violates regularity.
The pivotal role of every $P_A$ is a compelling reason to collect choice data from every \menu{} of a universe, if one wants to test regularity for some domain.

The next condition, the Block-Marschak inequalities, is relevant because it is not only necessary but also sufficient for the random utility condition.
A random choice model $P$ on a universe $\univ$ satisfies the Block-Marschak inequalities if and only if for all non-empty $A \subseteq U$ and all $x \in A$,
	\begin{equation}\label{e:BM}
		\sum_{B \colon A \subseteq B \subseteq U} (-1)^{|B \backslash A|} P_B(x) \geq 0,
	\end{equation}
where $|B\backslash A|$ is the number of items in $B$ that are not also in $A$.
($\backslash$ is the set difference operator and $|\cdot|$ denotes cardinality.)
This condition is far less interpretable than regularity, although see \citet[p. 713]{BarbPatt86} for an interpretation of the sum in \eqref{e:BM} for given $A$ and $x$, in the case that the full set of conditions holds.
\citet{BlocMars60} show that the Block-Marschak inequalities are necessary for the random utility condition and \citet{Falm78} shows they are sufficient.
Given the equivalence of the random utility condition and the Block-Marschak inequalities, we will henceforth refer to the condition using the more concise and familiar term, random utility.
For the random utility condition, as for regularity, each $P_A$ is pivotal: since regularity is necessary for random utility, the same change in $P_A$ that breaks regularity also breaks the random utility condition.

The third and final condition we consider is the multiplicative inequality, introduced by \citet{SattTver76}, who claim ``it has some appeal as a principle of individual choice behaviour''.
It states that the probability of choosing an option $x$ from a union $A \cup B$ of two \menus{} is at least as great as the probability of choosing $x$ from $A$ and then again from $B$ in independent trials. 
Suppose, for example, a random choice model $P$ on the universe of the ``Latitude'' domain satisfies the multiplicative inequality.
Then the probability of choosing Warsaw from the \menu{} of Warsaw, London and Vancouver must be at least as great as the joint probability of two independent events: choosing Warsaw over London in pairwise choice and then choosing Warsaw again in pairwise choice, this time over Vancouver.
In the experiment, 17 out of 40 participants chose Warsaw from the tripleton menu, 22 out of 40 chose Warsaw over London and 31 out of 40 chose Warsaw over Vancouver.
Since $0.425 = \hat{P}_{\{a,b,c\}}(a) < \hat{P}_{\{a,b\}}(a) \hat{P}_{\{a,c\}}(a) = 0.42625$, the system of choice {\em proportions} $\hat P$ violates the sample analogue of $P_{\{a,b,c\}}(a) \geq P_{\{a,b\}}(a) P_{\{a,c\}}(a)$, one of the multiplicative inequalities.
A formal test of the multiplicative inequality (a condition on choice {\em probabilities}) will take into account sampling variation and the full set of inequalities.
We perform such tests in Section \ref{s:global_tests} below.

Formally, a random choice model $P$ satisfies the {\em multiplicative inequality} if and only if for all $x \in A, B \subseteq \univ$,
\[
  P_{A \cup B}(x) \geq P_A(x) P_B(x).
\]
\citet{SattTver76} show that it is a testable implication of the independent random utility model and also of the Elimination by Aspects (EBA) model described in \citet{Tver72b} and \citet{Tver72a}.
\citet{McCaMarl24} show that any system of binary probabilities can be extended to a random choice model in such a way that the random choice model satisfies the multiplicative inequality.
As with regularity, each $P_A$ is pivotal.

The conditions we consider here are just three examples among the many that have been studied.
\citet{Fish99} documents these and many conditions on choice probabilities, including ones that have been proposed as reasonable assumptions about choice behaviour and some that are testable implications of different classes of random choice models.
See \citet{LuceSupp65} and \citet{McCaMarl13} for illustrations of the logical relationships among a set of conditions that includes the three outlined here and various kinds of stochastic transitivity.

\subsection{Individual- and population-level choice}\label{s:aggregation}

Most experiments involve a sequence of choice trials, where some \menu{} is presented at each trial.
We distinguish two different types of repeated choice behaviour.
We will qualify choice behaviour as {\em individual}-level when it consists of the repeated choices of a single individual and {\em population}-level when a participant is chosen at random from a population at each trial.
Hybrid experiments are possible, but we are not concerned with these here.
We have seen that our experiment has a population-level design.

A random choice model can be used to describe either individual- or population-level behaviour, but we should not expect the same kinds of model to apply to both.
For example, we would expect more heterogeneity in population-level choice, and thus fewer choice probabilities close to zero or one.
Whether or not a condition like regularity applies to individual-level choice and whether or not it applies to  population-level choice may both be of interest, but they are two distinct scientific questions.

That said, the two questions are sometimes related, through the following aggregation property: if the random choice model for all individuals in a population satisfies the condition, the model describing the population also satisfies the condition.
Expressed in contrapositive form to make plain the implications for testing, if the condition is violated for a population, then it must be violated by at least one individual.

Some, but not all, conditions have this aggregation property.
Fortunately, a simple mathematical test tells us whether or not any given condition does or does not.
Define $\Delta$ as the set of all random choice models and $\Lambda$ as the subset of models satisfying a given condition of interest.
Then the condition satisfies the aggregation property if and only if $\Lambda$ is a convex set.
This is because the population random choice model is a mixture---where random sampling from the population provides the mixing distribution---of the various individual random choice models.
If all individual random choice models lie in the same convex set, then the mixture, a convex combination, must also.
Conversely, if the region where the condition holds is not convex, we can find two individual random choice models that satisfy the condition such that the random choice model for the population of two does not.

We can see that regularity satisfies the aggregation property by verifying the convexity of the region where regularity holds.
The regularity inequalities are all linear in choice probabilities, and so the regularity region is the intersection of the convex set $\Delta$ and many half-spaces, also convex.
Therefore, the region is convex and regularity satisfies the aggregation property.
Likewise, the Block-Marschak inequalities are also linear in choice probabilities and so the random utility condition also satisfies the aggregation property.
The multiplicative inequality is not linear, however, and \citet{McCaMarl24} show that the region in which it holds is not convex.
Therefore it does not satisfy the aggregation property.

Another issue concerning individual- and population-level choice is the assumption, which we maintain throughout, that observed choices are statistically independent and for each \menu{}, identically distributed.
There are good reasons to be sceptical of this assumption for individual-level choice.
Participants may be learning or their attention may be waning during the course of an experiment.
The hypothesis is more plausible for population-level choice, where variation across observations comes from random sampling and not from changes in the behaviour of any individual.

\subsection{Context effects}\label{s:context_effects}

Context effects are examples of choice behaviour that are regarded as anomalous because they violate stochastic choice conditions that are either considered plausible or implied by important classes of discrete choice models.
To provide challenging environments for stochastic choice conditions, we deliberately design twelve of the choice domains to be conducive to one or more of three well-documented context effects: the attraction, similarity and compromise effects.
Here we review the effects and give some examples.
In Appendix \ref{s:domains}, where we describe all of the choice domains in detail, we describe how those twelve domains are designed to be conducive to these effects.
Section \ref{s:context_test} gives an empirical illustration where we test directly for an attraction effect in one of the domains.

The three context effects can be seen as contingent conditions on choice probabilities, conditions predicted to hold when the items in question are in certain relationships with each other.
In the example in the introduction, the condition predicted to hold is that the probability of choosing Vancouver goes up when Seattle is added to the \menu{}.
The prediction is contingent on Seattle being clearly ``inferior to'' (i.e. obviously further south than) Vancouver.
We will describe seminal versions of each of the three effects.
Other versions of these context effects have been proposed, and they have different aggregation properties and different implications on whether choice probabilities can satisfy regularity, the random utility condition, or other conditions not considered here, such as \citeauthor{Kran67}'s (\citeyear{Kran67}) simple scalability.
See \citet{StobMarlMcCaTurn23} for a comprehensive discussion.

The attraction effect (sometimes known as the asymmetric dominance effect) was introduced by \citet{HubePaynPuto82}, and pertains to choice environments with three items and some dominance relation over these items.
In one experimental design from \citet{HubePaynPuto82}, items are six-packs of beer with two attributes, price and quality rating.
A six-pack dominates another when its price is lower and its quality higher at the same time.
The three items are a ``target'' $x$, a ``competitor'' $y$ and a ``decoy'' $z$; $x$ dominates $z$, but $y$ does not; neither $x$ nor $y$ dominates the other.
In the six-pack example, the target $x$ has a price of $\$1.80$ and a rating of 50.
The competitor $y$ has a higher price ($\$2.60$) but also a higher rating (70) than the target, so neither dominates the other.
The decoy $z$ has a higher price ($\$2.20$) than the target and a lower rating (40).
The target dominates the decoy because it is cheaper and has a higher rating.
Importantly, the competitor does not dominate the decoy; this establishes an asymmetry between the target and the competitor.
The effect is said to occur when the probability of choosing the target goes up when the decoy is added to the pair of target and competitor; that is, when $P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x) > \bin{x}{y}$.
This condition is inconsistent with regularity, since one of the regularity inequalities is $\bin{x}{y} \geq P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x)$; and the random utility condition, because regularity is a necessary condition for random utility.
The ``Beer'' domain of our experiment is based on this design, but includes five items (and prices updated from 1982).

We also include domains where the dominance relation is not based on explicit levels of attributes.
One example is from the ``Latitude'' domain, a configuration with Vancouver as target, Warsaw as competitor and Seattle as decoy.
Two other configurations feature London and Paris, respectively, as competitor in place of Warsaw.

\citet{Tver72a} introduced the similarity effect, also pertaining to choice environments with three items.
Here, there is a similarity relation among the choice items under consideration.
Our ``Dots'' domain is similar to one of the three experimental designs in \citet{Tver72a} and includes boxes with 320, 310, 300, 290 and 280 points, labelled $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and $e$, respectively.
The prompt question is ``Which one of the following boxes do you think has the greatest number of points?''
We can define a similarity relation that considers two adjacent boxes in this list as similar.
The similarity effect occurs if there are items $x$, $y$ and $z$ such that $x$ and $y$ are similar, $x$ and $z$ are not similar and $y$ and $z$ are not similar and choice probabilities satisfy
\[
  \bin{x}{z} > \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(z)}
  \quad \mbox{and} \quad
  \bin{y}{z} > \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(z)}.
\]
The inequality on the left holds if adding the option $y$, similar to the target option $x$, to the \menu{} $\{x,z\}$ lowers the {\em relative} probability of choosing $x$, compared to $z$.
The inequality on the right is similar, except that $y$ is the target option and $x$ is the similar option.
The tripleton set $\{a, b, e\}$ is a candidate for the similarity effect, with $a$ and $b$ as the similar items and $e$ as the dissimilar item.
Likewise, $\{a, d, e\}$ is also a candidate, with $d$ and $e$ as the similar items.

\citet{Simo89} introduces a strong form of the compromise effect and \citet{TverSimo93} introduce a weaker version.
They pertain to choice environments where items can be described in terms of a set of real-valued attributes.
An item $y$ is {\em between} items $x$ and $z$ if two conditions hold: first, that for each attribute, $y$ has a level of that attribute intermediate between the levels of $x$ and $z$; and second, that each of $x$ and $z$ has the highest level of at least one attribute.
The weak effect is said to occur when $y$ is between $x$ and $z$ and the following inequalities, from page 1183 in \citet{TverSimo93}, hold:
\[
  \bin{y}{x} < \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x)}
  \quad \mbox{and} \quad
  \bin{y}{z} < \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(z)}.
\]
The inequality on the left holds if adding the option $z$ to the \menu{} $\{x,y\}$, making $y$ a ``compromise'' between $x$ and $z$, raises the relative probability of choosing $y$, compared to $x$.
The inequality on the right is similar, except that $x$ is added to $\{y,z\}$, again making $y$ a ``compromise'' between $x$ and $z$.
Our ``Beer'' domain includes three six-packs where a betweenness relation, relative to the price and quality rating attributes, applies.

The similarity and compromise effects are compatible with the conditions we consider in this paper, but not with others that may be of interest, such as the constant ratio rule described by \citet{Tver72b}.
See \citet{StobMarlMcCaTurn23} for details; different versions of context effects may differ in terms of their compatibility with different conditions.

\section{Experimental design}\label{s:design}

Here we describe the experiment, pointing out design features that serve the objective of testing abstract stochastic choice conditions across a wide variety of choice environments.

\subsection{Selection of choice domains}

An important objective is to cover a wide variety of choice environments.
For this reason we chose domains to span several qualitatively different kinds of choice, including consumer choice, aesthetic preference, factual judgment and perceptual discrimination.
For example, the ``Beer'' domain mentioned above mimics domains used in consumer choice experiments where different items have different levels of various attributes.
In the ``Juice'' domain, participants choose among different kinds of juice.
In some domains, participants make judgments, such as the ``Sentences'' domain, where they make relative judgments about grammaticality.
In some domains, like the ``Dots'' domain described above, choices involve perception.
Our experiment features $32$ choice domains in total, each domain consisting of a universe of five related choice items.

Table \ref{t:domains} shows the names of the choice domains, together with the prompt (or an excerpt of the prompt, with excluded text indicated by an ellipsis) used to elicit a response from the participant.

\subsection{A population-level full-\menu{} design}\label{s:design1040}

Many of the stochastic choice conditions one might want to test have the property that for each menu $A$, the probability vector $P_A$ is pivotal, in the sense defined in Section \ref{s:framework}.
This is true for the three conditions we have considered in this paper.
Because observed choices from any \menu{} have the potential to provide evidence against these conditions, it is important to observe choices from every \menu{} of a domain.
We have settled on a purely population-level design, to ensure that for each domain, choices are plausibly independent and, \menu{} by \menu{}, identically distributed.
For these reasons, each participant sees exactly one \menu{} from each domain, exactly once; and each \menu{} of each domain is seen by several participants.

Here are the details of the design.
There are $J=32$ choice domains, each with a 5-item universe.
For each domain, there are $I=26$ \menus{} (binary and larger subsets of the five-item universe), for a total of $JI=832$ \menus{}.
Since each participant faces exactly one \menu{} from each domain, once, the number of choice trials for each participant is the same as the number of domains, $J=32$.

Participant assignments were generated in blocks of 26.
Within each block and domain, the 26 participants were randomly assigned one-to-one to the 26 \menus{}, with assignments made independently across blocks and domains.
The intended design called for 40 complete blocks, yielding $40 \times 26 = 1040$ participants and exactly 40 observations per \menu{}.
However, two participants from the next block began the survey before the target had been reached and completed it afterward.
Their choices were retained for analysis.
Consequently, in each domain, two \menus{} were presented 41 times and the remaining 24 \menus{} were presented 40 times.
Because every participant faced exactly one \menu{} in each of the $J=32$ domains, the realized dataset contains $1042 \times 32 = 33,344$ trials.

\subsection{Other design features}

Participants are constrained to choose exactly one option from each \menu{}.
For some domains, participants select radio buttons and in others, they select pictures, which are then identified by a border.

\section{Bayesian models and inference}\label{s:models}

In Section \ref{s:framework}, we defined a random choice model $P$ on a universe $U$ of items.
The assumption that choices are independent, and for each \menu{} identically distributed, completes the description of the data generating process for data structured like ours.
We will denote by $N$ the experimental data as a random vector, before observation.
We will denote by $n$ a generic possible value of the data vector and by $n_{\mathrm{obs}}$ the realized data vector we observe.
The data generating process gives the conditional distribution of $N$ given $P$.

We use Bayesian inference to learn about plausible values of the unknown $P$ after observing $N = n_{\mathrm{obs}}$.
Because the goal is to test stochastic choice conditions, we conduct inference directly on the space of all random choice models and not a reduced-dimension parametric class.
That said, because there are a finite number of \menus{} and options, $P$ is a finite dimensional parameter vector.
For a universe of size five, there are 75 choice probabilities subject to 26 adding-up constraints, leaving 49 free parameters.
The parameter space, which we denote by $\Delta$, is the set of all random choice models; it consists of all values of the vector $P$ consistent with the axioms of probability.

Bayesian inference requires a prior distribution for $P$.
Once we have this, we have a joint distribution of $P$ and $N$ and we can derive, in principle, the posterior distribution of $P$ (i.e. the conditional distribution of $P$ given $N$) and the marginal distribution of $N$.
We will call the unknown $P$, completed by a prior distribution over $P$, a {\em Bayesian random choice model}.
The use of the word ``model'' here is consistent with its use in the term ``random choice model''.
Just as a particular known random choice model describes an unconditional distribution for $N$, a Bayesian random choice model implies an unconditional distribution for $N$.

Our main inferential objective is testing stochastic choice conditions using data, which in a Bayesian context is an exercise in model comparison.
To test a given condition, we will compare two Bayesian random choice models, an {\em encompassing} model where no restrictions are placed on $P$ beyond the axioms of probability, with a {\em constrained} model, where $P$ is restricted to the set of random choice models that satisfy the condition.
To do this we will use two different kinds of prior distribution.
The encompassing model is a Bayesian random choice model where the prior distribution of $P$ does not rule out any random choice models {\em a priori}; that is, the prior has full support on $\Delta$.
The constrained model is a Bayesian random choice model where the prior distribution is the truncation of the prior distribution of the encompassing model to the region where the condition holds.
Throughout the paper, $\Lambda$ denotes the subset of random choice models satisfying the condition under consideration; each tested hypothesis has the form \(P\in\Lambda\).

The standard summary statistic for a comparison of two Bayesian models is the Bayes factor.
If two Bayesian random choice models, $M$ and $M'$, are both considered equally likely before seeing the data, the Bayes factor in favour of $M'$ over $M$ gives the posterior odds ratio between them, $\Pr[M'|N=n_{\mathrm{obs}}]/\Pr[M|N=n_{\mathrm{obs}}]$.
So for example, if the two models are equally likely to begin with and the Bayes factor is 2, the posterior probability assigned to model $M'$ is twice as high as that assigned to $M$.

The Bayes factor in favour of $M'$ over $M$ is defined as a ratio of marginal likelihoods:
\[
  \mathrm{BF} \equiv
  \frac{f_{M'}(n_\mathrm{obs})}{f_M(n_\mathrm{obs})},
\]
where $f_M(n)$ is the marginal probability mass function of the data vector under model $M$, and $f_{M'}(n)$ is the same under $M'$.
Evaluating $f_M(\cdot)$ at the observed data $n_\mathrm{obs}$ gives a measure of the prior predictive performance of $M$ for the observed data.
Because it averages over all parameter values rather than evaluating a model's predictive performance at a fitted estimate, it measures predictive performance on unseen data.

For the special case when $M$ is an encompassing model and $M'$ is a constrained model obtained by truncating the prior distribution of $M$ to the region $\Lambda$ of $\Delta$ where a condition of interest holds, the Bayes factor reduces to the following intuitively satisfying form:
\begin{equation}\label{e:BF}
  \mathrm{BF} = \frac{\Pr_M[P \in \Lambda|N = n_\mathrm{obs}]}{\Pr_M[P \in \Lambda]}.
\end{equation}
The numerator is the posterior probability, under the encompassing model $M$, of the hypothesis $P \in \Lambda$, the hypothesis that the condition holds.
As such, the numerator measures the compatibility of the condition with the observed data $n_\mathrm{obs}$.
The denominator, the prior probability of the same hypothesis under the encompassing model, is an inverse measure of model parsimony; the lower the denominator, the more restrictive the condition and the more parsimonious the constrained model.
See \citet{KlugHoij07} for details.
For further explanation of Bayes factors, see \citet{Berg85} or \citet{BernSmit94}.

The ratio in \eqref{e:BF} also suggests a practical way of computing numerical estimates of Bayes factors.
Since the probability of an event is just the expectation of an indicator function for the event, we can estimate numerator and denominator using Monte Carlo methods for estimating expectations.
In the next section we turn to some empirical analyses, where we report Bayes factors estimated numerically in this way in order to compare various Bayesian random choice models.

\subsection{Tests of context effects using Bayes factors}\label{s:context_test}

Here we provide a worked example showing how to test the hypothesis that a context effect holds in a given situation.
We consider six possible configurations of target, competitor and decoy in the ``Latitude'' domain, based on the dominance relation that puts Vancouver over Seattle, as proposed in Section \ref{s:intro}, and London over Paris.
Each row of Table \ref{t:asy_dom_table} describes one configuration, provides the relevant choice counts and reports test results for the hypothesis that the effect holds.
The first three columns identify the target (T), competitor (C) and decoy (D) options that define the configuration.
The fourth column gives $n_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ and $n_{\{T,C\}}(C)$, the number of participants choosing the target and competitor, respectively, from the \menu{} of these two options.
The fifth column gives $n_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, $n_{\{T,C,D\}}(C)$ and $n_{\{T,C,D\}}(D)$, the number of participants choosing the target, competitor and decoy, respectively, from the \menu{} of these three options.
We see that the number of people choosing Vancouver goes up in the presence of Seattle, the decoy, when the competitor is either London or Paris.
This is suggestive evidence for two attraction effects.

Observations from menus consisting of the proposed target and decoy options provide some independent evidence on the plausibility of the two dominance relations, and these suggest that Seattle is a more convincing decoy for Vancouver than Paris is for London.
Of the participants who saw the menu of Vancouver and Seattle, 36 chose Vancouver and 4 chose Seattle.
Of those who saw the menu of London and Paris, only 32 chose London and 8 chose Paris.

For each configuration, the hypothesis is that the probability of choosing the target increases when the decoy is added to the menu.
The analysis therefore reduces to inference about two unknown probabilities and whether one exceeds the other.
We use an encompassing model with a prior distribution where all probability vectors are independent and uniformly distributed.
In particular, the probability vectors of interest, $P_{\{T,C\}} = (P_{\{T,C\}}(T), P_{\{T,C\}}(C))$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}} = (P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T), P_{\{T,C,D\}}(C), P_{\{T,C,D\}}(D))$ are independent and each is uniformly distributed on the region where its elements are non-negative and add to one.
The hypothesis we wish to test is that the attraction effect holds, or equivalently, that $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T) > P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$.
Taking $\Lambda$ to be the region of $\Delta$ where $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T) > P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$, the constrained model has a prior distribution for $P$ that is the truncation of the prior distribution in the encompassing model to the region $\Lambda$.

We use standard results for the Dirichlet-multinomial and beta-binomial models (see for example \citet{gelman:95}) to analytically derive the posterior distribution of $P_{\{T,C\}}$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}$ under the encompassing model.
In the following derivation, we denote by $\mathrm{Be}(\alpha,\beta)$ the beta distribution with shape parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
The prior independence of $P_{\{T,C\}}$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}$ and the assumed independence of participants' choices imply the posterior independence of $P_{\{T,C\}}$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}$.
The uniform distributions of $P_{\{T,C\}}$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}$ imply that the prior distribution of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1,1)$ and that that of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1,2)$.
Then the posterior distribution of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1 + n_{\{T,C\}}(T), 1 + n_{\{T,C\}}(C))$ and that of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1 + n_{\{T,C,D\}}(T), 2 + n_{\{T,C,D\}}(C) + n_{\{T,C,D\}}(D))$.

The test for the attraction effect is based on the Bayes factor in favour of the constrained model over the encompassing model, computed as  $\Pr[P \in \Lambda|N = n_{\mathrm{obs}}]/\Pr[P \in \Lambda]$, the ratio of posterior to prior probability of the effect under the encompassing model.
We computed $\Pr[P \in \Lambda] = \tfrac{1}{3}$ analytically.
We estimated $\Pr[P \in \Lambda|N = n_{\mathrm{obs}}]$ numerically with Monte Carlo simulation methods, using Rao-Blackwellization to reduce variance.
We computed numerical standard errors, which measure the standard deviation of simulation error.

Column six of Table \ref{t:asy_dom_table} gives numerical estimates of $\Pr[P \in \Lambda|N = n_{\mathrm{obs}}]$, the posterior probability of the attraction effect under the encompassing model.
All numerical standard errors are less than 0.0001.
The final two columns give the Bayes factor (BF), equal to the ratio $\Pr[P \in \Lambda|N=n_{\mathrm{obs}}]/\Pr[P \in \Lambda]$, and its natural logarithm.

The evidence favours the attraction effect for the three cases where the target is Vancouver and the decoy is Seattle.
In contrast, it favours the encompassing model for the three cases where the target is London and the decoy is Paris.

We can use the guidelines recommended by \citet{KassRaft95} to classify the strength of the evidence provided by the Bayes factors.
They classify log Bayes factors between 0 and 1 as ``not worth more than a bare mention'', those between 1 and 3 as providing ``positive evidence'', those between 3 and 5 as providing ``strong evidence'' and those above 5 as providing ``very strong evidence''.
We will adopt these categories throughout the paper, but refer to the first two as giving ``weak'' and ``moderate'' evidence.

By this reckoning, there is moderate evidence in favour of the attraction effect when the target is Vancouver, the competitor is Paris and the decoy is Seattle and moderate evidence against the attraction effect when the target is London, the decoy is Paris and the competitor is either Vancouver or Seattle.

We can also consider joint hypotheses, such as the hypothesis that the attraction effect holds in all three cases where the target is Vancouver and the decoy is Seattle.
This hypothesis consists of three inequalities and the log Bayes factor in favour of it is the sum of the individual log Bayes factors, or 2.253.
This is stronger evidence, but still not strong according to the standards of \citet{KassRaft95}.
The log Bayes factor in favour of the joint hypothesis that the attraction effect holds in all three cases where the target is London and the decoy is Paris is -3.744, which implies strong evidence against this joint hypothesis.

\subsection{Tests of three global abstract stochastic choice conditions}\label{s:global_tests}

Regularity, the random utility condition and the multiplicative inequality are also joint hypotheses.
However, the number of inequalities involved is much greater.
Regularity, for example, consists of 70 distinct and non-redundant inequalities when the universe consists of five items.

It is natural to choose a prior distribution where the $P_A$ are mutually independent across \menus{}.
We did this above when we tested for multiple attraction effects.
\citet{CavaDavi14}, \citet{DaviBrowCava15}, \citet{MyunKaraIver05} and \citet{ZwilCavaRege11} do the same for testing simpler and less restrictive conditions than the three we investigate here.

Although the simplicity of such a prior is in its favour, some kinds of prior correlation between choice probabilities on different \menus{} are intuitively compelling.
Suppose you observe choices only from the \menu{} $\{a,b,c\}$.
You don't know what the options $a$, $b$ and $c$ are but see that many more people choose $a$ than $b$.
Now you appraise choice probabilities on the smaller set $\{a,b\}$.
We suspect most people would put more weight on higher values of the probability $P_{\{a,b\}}(a)$ than they would before observing the choices from $\{a,b,c\}$; this, despite not knowing what the options are and having no direct evidence from observing choices from $\{a,b\}$.
This is consistent with Bayesian reasoning, but not when $P_{\{a,b\}}$ and $P_{\{a,b,c\}}$ are deemed {\em a priori} independent.

Furthermore, computational problems arise when we test our three conditions using a prior where the $P_A$ are independent.
For a universe of five items, the prior probabilities of these conditions are so tiny that the amount of simulation required to measure them using straightforward simulation would be prohibitively high.
The low prior probability of these conditions is a measure of how restrictive they are, compared with, for example, conditions on binary choice probabilities studied in the work cited above.

\citet{McCaMarl13} address both these issues by introducing a parametric prior distribution for $P$ where the $P_A$ are dependent.
For testing abstract stochastic choice conditions, the prior has some attractive properties, such as invariance to relabelling choice items.
Following \citet{McCaMarl14}, we used this class of priors for our empirical analysis.
In order to describe a prior sensitivity analysis we do below and to explain a difference between our analysis here and that in \citet{McCaMarl14}, we digress to describe the parameters of this prior distribution.

The prior has two parameters, $\lambda \in [0,1]$ and $\alpha > 0$.
The $\lambda$ parameter controls the degree of dependence of choice probabilities across \menus{}.
When $\lambda = 0$, the $P_A$ are mutually independent.
Thus, if you adopt a prior where $\lambda=0$, you learn nothing about $P_{\{a,b\}}(a)$, even if you observe a thousand people out of a thousand choosing $a$ from $\{a,b,c\}$.
As $\lambda$ increases, the prior probability of regularity and the random utility condition both increase.
The marginal distribution of each $P_A$ does not depend on $\lambda$, but correlations of choice probabilities across \menus{} do.
For example, the correlation between $P_{\{a,b,c\}}(a)$ and $P_{\{a,b\}}(a)$ increases in $\lambda$, even though their two marginal distributions do not depend on $\lambda$.
The prior is constructed so that at the maximal value $\lambda=1$, the probabilities of regularity and the random utility condition are both one.

The $\alpha$ parameter governs the degree to which choice probabilities concentrate near zero or one, with lower values of $\alpha$ corresponding to more probability mass near zero or one.
Thus, low values of $\alpha$ are associated with more extreme choice probabilities and high values of $\alpha$ with less extreme choice probabilities.
\iffalse
For $\alpha$ less than the number of options in a \menu{} $A$, the prior density is unbounded as any choice probability approaches one.
For $\alpha$ greater, there is a prior mode at the uniform discrete distribution on $A$, where all options are equally likely to be chosen.
As $\alpha$ increases, more prior probability mass concentrates near this mode.

For example, Figure \ref{f:bcp} illustrates how the prior density of any binary choice probability varies with $\alpha$.
For $\alpha < 2$, the density is unbounded, approaching infinity as the probability gets close to zero or one.
For $\alpha = 2$, the probability is uniformly distributed on the interval $[0,1]$.
For $\alpha > 2$, there is a mode at $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and as $\alpha$ increases, the variance decreases.

While we use the same family of priors here that we did for previous analyses in \citet{McCaMarl14} and \citet{McCaStobMarlParkBrow20}, some details are different.
The main reason is that the previous papers study individual-level choice and the current paper studies population-level choice.
We expect heterogeneity across individuals.
Therefore we anticipate more choice variability and thus less extreme choice probabilities at the population level.
For this reason, we favour higher values of $\alpha$ and therefore less prior probability mass for choice probabilities near zero and one, compared with those previous papers.

As in these previous papers, we conducted a prior sensitivity analysis.
Here, we tested each stochastic choice condition using three different encompassing models, $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$, which differ only in terms of the prior distribution of $\lambda$.
$M_1$ favours lower values of $\lambda$ than $M_2$ and $M_3$ favours higher values.
The priors were chosen to represent progressively stronger prior belief in dependence of choice probabilities across \menus{}.
The prior probability of the random utility condition is $0.002$ under $M_1$, $0.051$ under $M_2$ and $0.228$ under $M_3$.
We provide details of the three prior specifications in Appendix \ref{s:prior}.

We used the posterior simulation and model comparison methods described in \citet{McCaMarl14} for the computation of all results reported in Section \ref{s:global_tests}.
For each choice domain, we computed log\footnote{All logarithms are natural (base $e$) logarithms.} Bayes factors in favour of the encompassing models $M_1$ and $M_2$, over the encompassing model $M_3$.
For each encompassing model, we computed log Bayes factors in favour of three constrained models, over the encompassing model.
Those constrained models are the ones obtained by truncating the prior density for the encompassing model to the regions where regularity, random utility and the multiplicative inequality, respectively, hold.

Recall that log marginal likelihoods measure a model's prior predictive performance.
Log Bayes factors, which compare prior predictive performance between two models, are the differences of their log marginal likelihoods.
Therefore we can compute the log Bayes factor of any of the twelve models over any other as a difference between two of the log Bayes factors mentioned above.

We report log Bayes factors for the twelve models, all relative to the encompassing model $M_3$.
The choice of $M_3$ as a reference model, which is the most favoured encompassing model for 31 out of the 32 domains, just serves to establish a level of zero on a scale of prior predictive performance; by definition, the log Bayes factor for $M_3$ relative to itself is zero.

There is a panel for each domain, and the vertical scale in each panel measures the log Bayes factor of models relative to $M_3$.
Each symbol represents a model; the higher the symbol, the greater the log Bayes factor relative to $M_3$, and the better the model's prior predictive performance.
Symbols in column $i$, $i=1,2,3$, represent models based on the encompassing model $M_i$.

Each of these log Bayes factors summarizes the evidence for a stochastic choice condition, using a particular encompassing model.
They are given by the vertical distance between a symbol and the circle in the same column.
So for example, the vertical distance between the triangle and the circle in column $1$ of the top left panel ``Male stars'' gives the evidence in favour of regularity, using the encompassing model $M_1$, for the Male stars domain.

Recall that variation in results among the three encompassing models is used for prior sensitivity analysis.
Comparisons of Bayes factors across columns illustrate how results depend on changes to the prior specification.

There are no crosses for the choice domains ``Latitude'', ``Layovers'' and ``Future'', regardless of the encompassing model.
This is because the posterior probability of the multiplicative inequality is too close to zero to measure, implying very strong evidence against that condition for those domains.
Evidence in support of the multiplicative inequality varies a lot across choice domains, from moderate evidence in favour to very strong evidence against.
In each case, evidence is fairly robust to the choice of encompassing model.

The evidence for regularity and the random utility condition tends to be weaker, but more consistent across choice domains.
In most cases, the log Bayes factors either favour the random utility condition and regularity over the encompassing models, or are very close to zero, representing little evidence either way.
Across domains and encompassing models, the evidence for the random utility condition is generally similar to that for regularity, which is implied by random utility.
When the two differ, the evidence for regularity is usually slightly stronger, indicating some evidence against the higher order features of random utility, but this evidence is quite weak.
For individual domains, the evidence in favour of regularity and the random utility condition are at best slight, but collectively, the evidence is stronger.

Figure \ref{f:multiple_BF} also allows us to compare two different ways of generating structure in choice probabilities: on the one hand, imposing a condition such as regularity or the random utility condition, and on the other, increasing prior dependence across \menus{} through $\lambda$.
We see that the variation in predictive performance of the random utility condition across encompassing models (height of the square, across columns, for a particular domain) is much less than the variation in predictive performance across the encompassing models themselves (height of the circle, across columns, for the same domain).
We interpret this as follows.
Increasing $\lambda$ induces coherence across \menus{}.
So does imposing the random utility condition, in its own way.
The results suggest that some of the predictive gains from imposing the random utility condition can be replicated by increasing $\lambda$.
The same observation applies to regularity.

\subsection{Data visualization for observed choice proportions}\label{s:simplices}

The analyses above summarize evidence through Bayes factors.
We conclude with a graphical representation of the observed choice proportions on all binary and ternary \menus{} of the ``Latitude'' domain.
While purely descriptive and not inferential, the representation can reveal patterns that suggest violations of regularity and the multiplicative inequality.
Corresponding representations for all 32 domains are available in the supplementary materials.

We apply a graphical representation introduced by \citet{McCaMarl13} for plotting a random choice model, for a universe of size three, as a set of four points in a Barycentric coordinate system.
The random choice model can always be the restriction to a universe of size three of a random choice model on a larger universe.
Because systems of observed choice {\em proportions} satisfy the same non-negativity and adding-up constraints that random choice models do, the same graphical representation can be used to plot the observed choice proportions for all the \menus{} of size two and three from the same set of three choice items.

Recall that for the ``Latitude'' domain, the universe of items is $\{a,b,c,d,e\}$ where $a$ is Warsaw, $b$ is London, $c$ is Vancouver, $d$ is Paris, and $e$ is Seattle.
Table \ref{t:latitude_table} tabulates observed choice counts and choice proportions for all \menus{} of this domain.

Figure \ref{f:latitude_figure} displays ten panels, one for each tripleton subset of $\{a,b,c,d,e\}$.
The panel for a given tripleton shows an equilateral triangle with each vertex labelled with one of the items of that subset.
Consider the single panel in the fourth row, for the tripleton $\trip \equiv \{c,d,e\}$, which we will use as an extended example.
This triple corresponds to the configuration of Vancouver ($c$), Paris ($d$) and Seattle ($e$) where we saw the most pronounced evidence for an attraction effect.
The vertices of the triangle are labelled $c$ (bottom right), $d$ (top) and $e$ (bottom left).
Four menus are subsets of $\trip$, $\{c,d\}$, $\{c,e\}$, $\{d,e\}$ and $\{c,d,e\}$; the choice proportions observed for these menus appear in rows 8, 15, 19 and 23 of Table \ref{t:latitude_table}, respectively.

We use the graphical representation introduced by \citet{McCaMarl13} to plot the observed choice proportions for these four menus, using an equilateral triangle as a Barycentric coordinate system.
It uses a single triangle to display all binary and ternary choice probabilities (or observed proportions) for a universe of three items.
For example, an arbitrary probability vector $(P_\trip(c),P_\trip(d),P_\trip(e))$ on $\trip$ is represented as the point with Barycentric coordinates $(P_\trip(c),P_\trip(d),P_\trip(e))$ in the triangle.
We have represented the vector $(\hat P_\trip(c), \hat P_\trip(d), \hat P_\trip(e)) = (\tfrac{26}{40}, \tfrac{14}{40}, \tfrac{0}{40}) = (0.65, 0.35, 0.00)$ of choice proportions observed in the experiment as a solid point.
There are two ways of locating this point on the triangle.
First, we can take vertices $c$, $d$ and $e$ as points on the plane of the page or screen.
Then the point $(\hat P_\trip(c),\hat P_\trip(d),\hat P_\trip(e))$ is the convex combination of the vertices $c$, $d$ and $e$---which have Barycentric coordinates $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$, respectively---with weights $0.65$, $0.35$ and $0.00$.
Thus, the solid point is at $0.65 c + 0.35 d$.
The second way relies on the fact that the triangle is equilateral.
Taking the triangle to have unit height, the point representing $(\hat P_\trip(c),\hat P_\trip(d),\hat P_\trip(e))$ is at a distance of $\hat P_\trip(c) = 0.65$ to the left side of the triangle, at a distance of $\hat P_\trip(d) = 0.35$ to the base and at a distance of $\hat P_\trip(e) = 0.00$ to the right side.
The upward sloping grid lines, the horizontal grid lines, and the downward sloping grid lines measure out the choice proportions of $c$, $d$ and $e$, respectively.

The hollow dots on the right, left and bottom sides of the triangle represent the choice proportions $\hat P_{\{c,d\}}(c) = \tfrac{17}{40} = 0.425$, $\hat P_{\{d,e\}}(d) = \tfrac{26}{40} = 0.65$ and $\hat P_{\{c,e\}}(c) = \tfrac{36}{40} = 0.9$, respectively.
For example, the hollow dot on the right side of the triangle is the convex combination of the vertices labelled $c$ and $d$, with weights $0.425$ and $0.575$ respectively.
Equivalently, the distance from this hollow dot to vertex $c$ and the distance to vertex $d$ are in the proportions 0.575 and 0.425.

The two triangles in the interior allow readers to detect, at a glance, if binary and ternary choice proportions for the tripleton set $\trip$ violate regularity or the multiplicative inequality.
(Strictly speaking, regularity and the multiplicative inequality are restrictions on choice probabilities, not proportions.
But a system of choice proportions has the same structure as a random choice model and belongs to the same space $\Delta$.
We define regularity and the multiplicative inequality for a system of choice proportions as the analogous conditions, with choice proportions replacing choice probabilities.)
Suppose that we know the binary choice proportions $\hat P_{\{c,d\}}(c) = \tfrac{17}{40} = 0.425$, $\hat P_{\{d,e\}}(d) = \tfrac{26}{40} = 0.65$ and $\hat P_{\{c,e\}}(c) = \tfrac{36}{40} = 0.90$, but we have not yet observed the ternary choice proportions $(\hat P_\trip(c), \hat P_\trip(d), \hat P_\trip(e))$.

The downward pointing solid triangle with the blue boundary is a cross-section of the region where regularity holds.
For the three given binary choice proportions (indicated by the three hollow dots) the solid triangle with the blue boundary is the set of possible ternary choice proportions consistent with regularity and the three observed binary choice proportions.
The observed ternary proportions (the solid dot) may or may not lie in the blue triangle; here they do not.
The blue triangle is defined by the regularity inequalities on $\trip$, which simplify to
$\hat P_\trip(c) \leq \min(\hat P_{\{c,d\}}(c), \hat P_{\{c,e\}}(c))$,
$\hat P_\trip(d) \leq \min(\hat P_{\{c,d\}}(d), \hat P_{\{d,e\}}(d))$ and
$\hat P_\trip(e) \leq \min(\hat P_{\{c,e\}}(e), \hat P_{\{d,e\}}(e))$.
The observed choice proportion vector from \menu{} $\trip$, $(\hat P_\trip(c), \hat P_\trip(d), \hat P_\trip(e))$, lies outside the triangle with the blue boundary because $\hat P_\trip(c) = 0.65$ is greater than $\hat P_{\{c,d\}}(c) = 0.425$; we conclude that the binary and ternary choice proportions on $\trip$ do not satisfy regularity.
It is important to remember that the observed choice proportions are subject to sampling variation and so the fact that choice proportions violate regularity does not constitute a rejection of the regularity hypothesis.
We are describing here a data visualization method and not the results of statistical tests.

Similarly, the upward pointing solid triangle with the red boundary is a cross-section of the region where the multiplicative inequality holds.
For the given binary choice proportions, this solid triangle is the set of ternary choice proportions consistent with the multiplicative inequality and the observed  binary choice proportions.
The multiplicative inequality on $\trip$ reduces to
$\hat P_\trip(c) \geq \hat P_{\{c,d\}}(c) \hat P_{\{c,e\}}(c)$ and
$\hat P_\trip(d) \geq \hat P_{\{c,d\}}(d) \hat P_{\{d,e\}}(d)$ and
$\hat P_\trip(e) \geq \hat P_{\{c,e\}}(e) \hat P_{\{d,e\}}(e)$.
The vector $(\hat P_\trip(c), \hat P_\trip(d), \hat P_\trip(e))$ of ternary choice proportions lies outside the red triangle and in particular, $\hat P_\trip(d) = 0.35$ is less than $\hat P_{\{c,d\}}(d) \hat P_{\{d,e\}}(d) = 0.575 \times 0.65 = 0.37375$.

\section{Conclusions}\label{s:conclude}

We have collected new experimental data featuring observations of population-level choice.
Several features of the experimental design make the data suitable for testing abstract stochastic choice conditions at the population level.
First, it includes a wide variety of choice domains.
Some choice domains involve subjective aesthetic judgments, on such matters as food and drink, art, music, politics and the grammaticality of sentences.
Other choice domains resemble choice domains that naturally occur in markets, such as those for mobile phone plans, hotel rooms, beer, cars and flights.
Still others involve options having an objective rank order.
Some choice domains have options that are distinguished only by the levels of numerical attributes, others do not.
Some domains are designed to be conducive to context effects, in order to stress-test conditions that are inconsistent with the effects.
This variety helps us and others to discover properties of random choice structures that hold widely.

Second, for each choice domain, we observed repeated choices from all \menus{} of a universe of five items, allowing us to test any condition by exposing every implication of it to empirical scrutiny.
This is particularly important for testing conditions like regularity, random utility and the multiplicative inequality, where every \menu{}-specific choice probability vector $P_A$ is pivotal, in the sense defined in Section \ref{s:conditions}.
Third, by arranging that every participant saw only one \menu{} from each choice domain, we obtained data on population choices that are plausibly independent and, \menu{} by \menu{}, identically distributed.

The interpretation and generalizability of the results are necessarily tied to these design features.
The data consist of population-level rather than individual-level choices, and implications for individual choice depend on the aggregation property discussed in Section \ref{s:aggregation}.
Participants were members of a Canadian online panel, so the results do not necessarily generalize to other populations, periods or elicitation methods.
Although the 32 domains are deliberately diverse, they are not exhaustive.

The experimental dataset we have collected complements earlier work, including the abstract choice models introduced in \citet{McCaMarl13} and the inferential methods described in \citet{McCaMarl14}.
The data, supported by these models and methods, serve as a testing ground for the conditions we have considered in this paper and any others that one may wish to investigate.
That said, the data are publicly available and stand alone, amenable to analysis using other models and methods.

Evidence in support of regularity and the random utility condition is fairly consistent across choice domains.
While the evidence in favour of these conditions for any one choice domain is modest, the direction of the evidence is robust to the choice of prior and the consistency of the evidence across diverse domains constitutes important evidence in favour of the broad applicability of these conditions.
While the literature on the attraction effect provides evidence against regularity and therefore random utility in special cases, these conditions hold up across the wide variety of choice domains we explore, including a few domains designed to be conducive to the attraction effect.
This is reassuring given the prevalence of random utility models in the literature.

Evidence regarding the multiplicative inequality, in contrast, varies considerably across domains.
There is very strong evidence against it for three domains, so we can conclude that it cannot be considered as a universal condition for population-level stochastic choice.
However, the multiplicative inequality does not have the aggregation property discussed in Section \ref{s:aggregation} so we cannot conclude that it is unsuitable for individual-level choice.
\citet{Tver72a} introduced the EBA class of models to accommodate the similarity effect, introduced in the same paper, but since the multiplicative inequality is a necessary condition for EBA models, there are population-level choice patterns that it cannot capture well.
At the same time, the multiplicative inequality is favoured more than the random utility condition in many domains, which raises the question of what common features these domains might have.
In future work we wish to investigate the multiplicative inequality in more detail, using a variety of data sources, complementing some theoretical work on that condition in \citet{McCaMarl24}.

The dataset can be reused to evaluate other conditions on probabilistic choice behaviour, including behavioural axioms and conditions implied by different competing theories. 
A better understanding of choice behaviour requires evidence not only about specific anomalies, but also the broad applicability of such conditions.
By providing a diverse population-level choice dataset, the present study aims to contribute to this endeavour.

\section{Outtakes}

To estimate the prior probability $\Pr[\Lambda]$, it would be possible to draw large samples from the prior distribution of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ and use the proportion of draws where $P_{\{T,C\}}(T) < P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ as a numerical estimate of $\Pr[\Lambda]$.
We could similarly estimate the posterior probability using the posterior distributions of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.

The experiment above was designed to elicit a context effect known as the attraction effect.
The effect pertains to choice environments with three items, a {\em target}, a {\em competitor} and a {\em decoy}, and a dominance relation over these items.
The target dominates the decoy but the competitor does not.
Neither the target nor the competitor dominate the other.
The effect is said to occur when the probability of choosing the target goes up when the decoy is added to the pair of target and competitor.
We propose Vancouver as target, Paris as competitor and Seattle as decoy, reasoning that it is obvious to participants that Vancouver is north of Seattle, but not obvious which of Vancouver and Paris is further north, nor which of Seattle and Paris is furthest north.

We must consider sampling variability to assess the strength of this evidence.

The experimental design delivers observations from every menu, allowing the measurement of all choice probabilities.

We observe exactly one choice of city from each of the 1040 participants.
Variation comes from sampling variation and not the variation of an individual's choice behaviour from trial to trial.

\subsection{Attraction effect inference}

We will apply a variance reduction technique called Rao-Blackwellization to obtain high numerical precision.
Denote the cumulative distribution function for $\mathrm{Be}(\alpha, \beta)$ by $F_{\mathrm{Be}}(\cdot;\alpha, \beta)$.
Then $F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1, 1)$, the prior cdf of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ evaluated at the random variable $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, is the conditional prior probability of $\Lambda$ given $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
Therefore $E[F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1, 1)]$ is the marginal prior probability $\Pr[\Lambda]$, where the expectation is taken with respect to the prior distribution of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
Similarly, $F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(T), 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(C))$ is the conditional {\em posterior} probability of $\Lambda$ given $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ and its posterior expectation is $\Pr[\Lambda|N]$.

We use the sample mean counterparts of these expectations to estimate them numerically.
To estimate $\Pr[\Lambda]$, we first draw a iid sample of size $M$ from the {\em prior} distribution of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
Denote the $m$'th draw by $P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
The standard strong law of large numbers for independent draws and bounded random variables ensures the following almost sure (a.s.) convergence:
\[
  \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1, 1)
  \overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \Pr[\Lambda].
\]
Similarly, for an iid sample $P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, $m=1,\ldots,M$, from the {\em posterior} distribution of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, the distribution $\mathrm{Be}(1 + N_T^{(3)}, 2 + N_C^{(3)} + N_D^{(3)}$, we have 
\[
  \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T);
  1 + N_T^{(2)}, 1 + N_C^{(2)})
  \overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \Pr[\Lambda|N].
\]
We estimated $\Pr[\Lambda]$ and $\Pr[\Lambda|N]$ using samples of size $M = 10^6$.
We computed the usual numerical standard errors of the estimators and found all to be less than 0.0003.

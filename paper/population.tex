\begin{abstract}
  We run an experiment covering 32 different choice domains, including fine art, cars and pizza.
  For each domain, there is a set of 5 related objects, and 26 \menus{}---the subsets of two or more objects.
  We measure population level choice probabilities by presenting each \menu{} to 40 participants and observing their choices of a single object.
  The purpose of the experiment is to allow tests of various conditions over choice probabilities, over a wide variety of choice domains. It is particularly well suited for testing conditions, like regularity, random utility and the multiplicative inequality, which constrain choice probabilities across all \menus{} of a domain.
	Each participant faces exactly one \menu{} from each domain; for between-subject designs like this, the assumption that choices are independent across trials, and on each \menu{}, identically distributed, is plausible.
	Participants are representative of the Canadian adult population.
	For each domain and each of the three conditions above, we report Bayes factors giving evidence for or against the condition.
	Evidence supports regularity and random utility, conditions that most discrete models satisfy, across all domains; for the multiplicative inequality, a condition satisfied by independent random utility models and the Elimination by Aspects model, evidence ranges from favorable to strongly unfavorable.
	\\
	Key words: Discrete choice, Stochastic choice, Choice experiment, Context effects, Random utility, Bayesian inference \\
	JEL codes: C11, C12, C15, C35, C91, D01, D90.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Discrete choice models, which describe the choices of decision makers from finite menus of options, are used extensively in economics, psychology, marketing, transportation research, health research and other disciplines.
We focus on models describing the choice of a single option from each of a sequence of \menus{}.
Most empirical applications seek to measure how attributes of choice options affect choice probabilities in particular cases; for example, how commute times and other features affect the probabilities of choosing various transportation options, in \citeasnoun{Trai78}.
Here, we contribute to a distinct and more abstract research agenda that collects evidence for or against, as the case may be, various testable conditions on choice probabilities, without regard to object attributes, across a wide variety of choice environments.
An example of such a condition is {\em regularity}, which states that for all \menus{} $A$ and $B$ from a universe of objects such that $A \subseteq B$, the probability of choosing $x \in A$ when \menu{} $A$ is presented is no less than the probability of choosing $x$ when $B$ is presented.
Most would find it counterintuitive if an option in a \menu{} were more likely to be chosen when another option is added to the \menu{}, whatever the choice domain and the attributes of the options in question.
One important objective of the broader research agenda this work is a part of is to find conditions that are empirically plausible across a wide variety of choice domains.
This is important for the development and refinement of descriptive theories of choice, which constrain choice probabilities in different ways; for example, most discrete choice models satisfy regularity.
To this end, we abstract away from object attributes and, for the purposes of statistical inference treat choice objects in a given universe as {\em a priori} indistinguishable: before observing choices among objects, all we know about an object is its index, allowing us to follow the object across all the \menus{} that include it.
In different applications, choice data observed over several trials may be a sequence of choices made by a single decision maker or the choices of a sample of decision makers randomly selected from a population.

This paper advances this abstract choice research agenda by contributing new experimental data and some preliminary empirical analysis.
The data serve the research agenda in several ways.
First, the experiment includes a wide variety of choice domains.
We observe participants choosing from \menus{} from 32 different domains, including fine art, cars and pizza.
One can use this data to see which choice patterns are more universal and which are more idiosyncratic.
Second, for each domain, we observe choices from every \menu{}; that is, from each set of at least two objects from the domain.
Each domain has five objects, and thus\footnote{From the $2^5 = 32$ subsets of a set of 5 objects, we subtract 5 singleton sets and the empty set.} 26 \menus{}.
For each \menu{} of each domain, we observe the choices of 40 different participants, allowing us to measure population choice probabilities with some precision.
For conditions such as regularity, which constrains choice probabilities across all \menus{} of a domain, measuring all choice probabilities allows us to expose all implications of the condition to possible falsification.\footnote{Here and elsewhere, we use the term falsification in a statistical, rather than a strict  Popperian, sense. We take it to mean strong and compelling statistical evidence, either Bayesian or frequentist, against a hypothesis.}
Third, the experiment features a strictly between-subject design, where each participant faces exactly one \menu{} from each domain.
For these designs, the standard assumption that choices are independent across trials, and on each \menu{} identically distributed, is plausible.

To demonstrate the kind of empirical analysis that we can perform using these data, we perform tests of three conditions for which the new data are particularly well suited: regularity, random utility and the multiplicative inequality.
We do this for each of the 32 choice domains of the experiment, using Bayesian methods developed in two previous papers.
\citeasnoun{McCaMarl13} develops non-parametric models, defined up the choice of a prior distribution, for systems of choice probabilities.
The paper also provides tools for detecting whether or not a given system of probabilities satisfies each of a variety of testable conditions.
\citeasnoun{McCaMarl14} provides methods for the simulation of choice probabilities from their posterior distribution, for these non-parametric models.
It also describes methods for testing conditions using Bayes factors.
Together, the two papers establish a testing ground for various conditions, not limited to those studied in those papers.

We emphasize that the conditions we test here are three examples among many, and it is beyond the scope of this paper to consider more than a few.
\citeasnoun{Fish99} documents many conditions, including conditions that have been proposed as reasonable assumptions about choice behaviour and conditions that are testable implications of different choice theories and choice models.
The context effects literature has proposed several conditions that are predicted to occur in specific contexts.
While we do not do any empirical analyses of context effects, we do discuss them here because many of the choice domains in the experiment are designed to elicit context effects.
Including these domains contributes to the objectives of providing a wide diversity of choice domains and allowing (future) investigations of context effects.

We also emphasize that we make these data public, allowing others to investigate other conditions, using whatever inferential methods they wish.

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $\univ = \{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ be a universe of choice objects.
When faced with a non-empty \menu{} $A \subseteq \univ$, an agent chooses a single object from $A$.
The probability that the agent chooses $x \in A$ is denoted $P_A(x)$.
A {\em random choice structure} (RCS) on $\univ$ is the complete specification of the system of choice probabilities $P_A(x)$, $x \in A \subseteq \univ$, and is denoted $P$.
We will write $\univ = \{a,b,c,d,e\}$ for a generic universe with five elements; for the ``Colours'' domain of the experiment, the prompt is ``Which one of the following colours do you like best?'', $a$ is Red, $b$ is Purple, $c$ is Pink, $d$ is Blue, and $e$ is Green.
One of the 26 menus is $\{a,b\}$; $P_{\{a,b\}}(a)$ and $P_{\{a,b\}}(b)$ are the probabilities of choosing Red and Purple from the menu consisting of these two options.
We learn something about these probabilities from the experiment: 21 participants chose Red and 19 chose Purple, as we can see in the first row of Table \ref{t:colours_table} of Section \ref{s:results}, which tabulates choice counts for each of the 26 \menus{} of the ``Colours'' domain.
Denote the observed proportion of times that an object $x$ is chosen from a \menu{} $A$, $x \in A \subseteq \univ$, by $\hat P_A(x)$.
For example, we compute the observed choice proportions $\hat P_{\{a,b\}}(a) = 0.525$ and $\hat P_{\{a,b\}}(b) = 0.475$ for the ``Colours'' domain.

Let $\Delta$ be the set of random choice structures; $\Delta$ is a subset of Euclidean space defined by the non-negativity of choice probabilites and the requirement that choice probabilities on each \menu{} add up to one.

We distinguish between two different interpretations of a RCS.
An {\em individual} RCS governs the choices of a single individual; a {\em population} RCS, those of a random sample of individuals from a population.
Note that if each individual in a population is governed by an individual RCS then the population RCS will be a convex combination of individual RCSs: each population choice probability mass function $P_A(\cdot)$, $A \subseteq \univ$, is a mixture---with random sampling from the population being the common mixing distribution---of the various individual probability mass functions $P_A(\cdot)$.

In situations where choice is repeated, we assume that choices are statistically independent across trials, and that for each \menu{} $A$, the same $P_A(\cdot)$ governs every choice from $A$.
Following others, we refer to these two assumptions together as the iid assumption; note that ``identically distributed'' applies separately to each \menu{} while ``independent'' applies globally.

There are good reasons to be sceptical of the iid assumption in experiments featuring within-subject or mixed within/between designs; participants may be learning or their attention may be waning during the course of the experiment.
While steps can be taken to attenuate these problems by limiting the number of repetitions of each \menu{} and by using distractor trials to make it more difficult for participants to recognize choice objects, and while we can test the assumptions to some extent, we can never be sure that the assumptions hold.
In a strictly between-subject design, however, with no communication between participants, the iid assumption is much more plausible.

\subsection{Testible conditions for discrete stochastic choice}\label{s:axioms}

Various axioms, conditions, constriants, hypotheses, properties, propositions and rules about probabilistic choice behaviour can be expressed as restrictions over the various choice probabilities of a RCS.
We have been using, and will continue to use, the term ``condition'' as a generic term to include all of these.
Historically, many of these conditions pertain only to binary choice probabilities.
See, for example, \citeasnoun{LuceSupp65} for various forms of stochastic transitivity and also the triangle inequality.

Given the nature of the experimental data we introduce in this paper, we will focus on the following three testible conditions, each of which relates choice probabilities across all \menus{} of a choice universe, not just the doubleton \menus{}.
A random choice structure $P$ for a universe $\univ$ of choices satisfies
\begin{description}
	\item[Reg] {\em regularity} if and only if for all $x \in A \subseteq B \subseteq \univ$,
	\[
		P_A(x) \geq P_B(x),
	\]
	\item[RU] {\em random utility} (or the {\em Block-Marschak inequalities}) if and only if for all non-empty
	$A \subseteq \univ$ and all $x \in A$,
	\begin{equation}
		\sum_{B \colon A \subseteq B \subseteq \univ} (-1)^{|B \backslash A|} P_B(x) \geq 0,
	\end{equation}
  \item[MI] the {\em multiplicative inequality} if and only if for all $x \in A, B \subseteq \univ$,
	\[
		P_{A \cup B}(x) \geq P_A(x) P_B(x).
	\]
\end{description}
If a random choice structure $P$ for the ``Colours'' domain satisfies Reg, then the probability of choosing Pink from a menu consisting of Red, Purple and Pink cannot be greater than the probability of choosing Pink from the doubleton menu of Purple and Pink.
The fact that six participants out of forty chose Pink from the first \menu{} and only three participants out of forty chose Pink from the second \menu{}, a strict subset of the first, means that the observed system of choice {\em proportions} violates Reg.
However, before rejecting the hypothesis that the population RCS violates Reg, we have to take into account sampling variability and the fact that the regularity condition consists of multiple inequalities.
We do exactly this in Section \ref{s:results}.

\citeasnoun{LuceSupp65} describe Reg and show that it is necessary for RU.
\citeasnoun{BlocMars60} show that the Block-Marschak inequalities are necessary for random utility and \citeasnoun{Falm78} shows they are sufficient.
Most discrete choice models used in applied work are random utility models, and thus they induce choice probabilities that satisfy the Reg and RU conditions.

If $P$ satisfies the multiplicative inequality, then the probability of choosing Purple from the tripleton menu of Red, Purple and Pink has to be at least as great as the joint probability of two independent events: choosing Purple over Red in pairwise choice and then choosing Purple over Pink in pairwise choice.
In the experiment, 15 out of 40 participants chose Purple from the tripleton menu, 19 out of 40 chose Purple over Red and 37 out of 40 chose Purple over Pink.
Since $0.375 = \hat{P}_{\{a,b,c\}}(a) < \hat{P}_{\{a,b\}}(a) \hat{P}_{\{a,c\}}(a) = 0.439375$, the system of choice {\em proportions} violates the MI, but again, a test of MI (for choice {\em probabilities}) will take into account sampling variation and the multiple inequalities.

The MI is due to \citeasnoun{SattTver76}, who show that it is a testable implication of the independent random utility model and also of the Elimination by Aspects (EBA) model described in \citeasnoun{Tver72b} and \citeasnoun{Tver72a}.
\citeasnoun{McCaMarl24} show that any system of binary probabilities can be extended to a complete random choice structure in such a way that the complete random choice structure satisfies the MI.
They also prove several results on the relationship between Reg and the MI.
See \citeasnoun{LuceSupp65} and \citeasnoun{McCaMarl13} for illustrations of the logical relationships among a set of conditions that includes these three and various kinds of stochastic transitivity.

It is easy to see that for each of the three conditions defined above, every choice probability vector $P_A(\cdot)$ appears in many constraints.
Moreover, each $P_A(\cdot)$ is {\em pivotal}, in the sense that for any given random choice structure satisfying the condition in question and an arbitrary \menu{} $A$, one can change $P_A(\cdot)$ to make the random choice structure violate the condition.
It is for this reason that to test these conditions, and others with this property, it is important to observe choice data from every \menu{} $A$.

It will be useful to frame the three conditions as defining subsets of the space $\Delta$ of random choice structures.
Let $\Lambda_{\mathrm{Reg}}$ be the subset of $\Delta$ where Reg holds; similarly, define $\Lambda_{\mathrm{RU}}$ and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{MI}}$ as the subsets of $\Delta$ where RU and the MI hold.
Since Reg and RU consist of linear inequalities in probabilities and $\Delta$ is convex, $\Lambda_{\mathrm{Reg}}$ and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{RU}}$ are both convex.

Whether or not an condition applies to a population RCS and whether or not it applies to individual RCSs are both of interest, but they are distinct questions.
Nonetheless, for ``convex'' conditions such as regularity and random utility, if the RCS for all individuals in a population satisfy the condition, then the population RCS must as well.
This is because the population RCS, being a convex combination of individual RCSs, must satisfy the condition whenever all the individual ones do.
Expressed in contrapositive form to make plain the implications for testing, if the condition is violated for a population, than it must be violated by at least one individual.

Other conditions are not convex in this sense.
In particular, \citeasnoun{McCaMarl24} show that $\Lambda_{\mathrm{MI}}$ is not convex, for any choice universe with at least three objects.
Thus, the MI can hold for every individual in a population but not the population itself.

\subsection{Context effects}

One of the objectives of our experimental design is to include a wide variety of choice domains.
To this end, we include choice domains designed to elicit anomalous behaviour, in the form of three well documented ``context effects'': the asymmetric dominance, similarity and compromise effects.
In Appendix \ref{s:domains}, where we describe the choice domains in detail, we describe how some of the domains (not limited to the examples in this section) are designed to elicit these effects.

Context effects can be seen as conditions on choice probabilities that are contingent, predicted to hold when the objects in question satisfy certain properties.
Here we describe seminal versions of each of the three effects.
Other versions of each of these context effects have been proposed, and they have different aggregation properties and different implications on whether systems of choice probabilities satisfy Reg, RU and other conditions not considered here, like \possessivecite{Kran67} simple scalability.
See \citeasnoun{StobMarlMcCaTurn23} for a comprehensive discussion.

The asymmetric dominance effect (sometimes known as the attraction effect) was introduced by \citeasnoun{HubePaynPuto82}, and pertains to choice environments with three objects and a dominance relation over these objects.
The objects are a ``target'' $x$, a ``competitor'' $y$ and a ``decoy'' $z$;
$x$ dominates $z$, but $y$ does not; neither $x$ nor $y$ dominates the other.
In one experimental design from \citeasnoun{HubePaynPuto82}, objects are six-packs of beer with two attributes, price and quality rating.
The target $x$ has a price of $\$1.80$ and a rating of 50.
The competitor $y$ has a higher price ($\$2.60$) but also a higher rating (70) than the target, so neither dominates the other, in the sense of having both a lower price and a higher rating.
The decoy $z$ has a higher price ($\$2.20$) than the target and a lower rating (40).
The target dominates the decoy because it is cheaper and has a higher rating.
The effect is said to occur when the probability of choosing the target goes up when the decoy is added to the pair of target and competitor; that is, when $P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x) > \bin{x}{y}$.
This condition constitutes evidence against regularity, since one of the regularity inequalities is $P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x) \leq \bin{x}{y}$, and random utility, because regularity is a necessary condition for random utility.
The ``Beer'' domain of our experiment is based on this design, but includes five objects (and prices updated from 1982).

We also include domains where the dominance relation is not based on explicit levels of attributes.
Take the ``Latitude'' domain, for example, and the objects Vancouver, Seattle and Paris in particular.
All three cities are between $47^\circ$ and $50^\circ$ north of the equator.
The prompt is ``Which of the following cities do you think is furthest north?''.
We propose Vancouver as target, Paris as competitor, and Seattle as decoy, reasoning that for participants, it is obvious that Vancouver is north of Seattle, but not which of Vancouver and Paris is furthest north nor which of Seattle and Paris is furthest north.
The effect occurs for the implied dominance relation if the probability of choosing Vancouver increases when Seattle (perhaps making Vancouver appear farther north) is added to the pair of Vancouver and Paris.
Of the 40 participants who saw the menu with Vancouver and Paris, 17 choose Vancouver and 23, Paris.
Of the 40 participants who saw the menu with Vancouver, Paris and Seattle, 26 chose Vancouver; 14, Paris; and none, Seattle.
The choice {\em proportions} are consistent with the asymmetric dominance effect and inconsistent with Reg and RU, but to test Reg and RU, we need to consider sampling variation and the multiple implications of these conditions, which we do in Section \ref{s:inference}.

\citeasnoun{Tver72a} introduces the similarity effect, defined up to the definition of a similarity relation on the choice objects under consideration.
The similarity effect defined there occurs if there are $x$, $y$ and $z$ such that $x$ and $y$ are similar, $x$ and $z$ are not similar and $y$ and $z$ are not similar, and choice probabilities satisfy
\[
	\bin{x}{z} > \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(z)}
	\quad \mbox{and} \quad
	\bin{y}{z} > \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(z)}.
\]
The inequality on the left holds if adding the object $y$, similar to the target object $x$, to the \menu{} $\{x,z\}$ lowers the {\em relative} probability of choosing $x$, compared to $z$.
The inequality on the right is similar, except that $y$ is the target object and $x$ is the similar object.
Our ``Dots'' domain, similar to one of the three experimental designs in \citeasnoun{Tver72a}, includes boxes with 320, 310, 300, 290 and 280 points, or $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and $e$, respectively.
The prompt question is ``Which one of the following boxes do you think has the greatest number of points?''
The triples $\{a, b, e\}$ and $\{a, d, e\}$ are candidates for eliciting similarity effects.

\citeasnoun{Simo89} introduce a strong form of the compromise effect and \citeasnoun{TverSimo93} introduce a weaker version.
They pertain to choice environments where objects can be described in terms of a set of real-valued attributes.
An object $y$ is {\em between} objects $x$ and $z$, denoted $x|y|z$, if two conditions hold: first, for each attribute, $y$ has a level of that attribute intermediate between the levels of $x$ and $z$; and second, that each of $x$ and $z$ have the highest level of at least one attribute.
The weak effect is said to occur when $x|y|z$ and the following inequalities, from page 1183 in \citeasnoun{TverSimo93}, hold:
\[
	\bin{y}{x} < \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(x)}
	\quad \mbox{and} \quad
	\bin{y}{z} < \frac{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y)}{P_{\{x,y,z\}}(y) + P_{\{x,y,z\}}(z)}.
\]
The inequality on the left holds if adding the object $z$ to the \menu{} $\{x,y\}$, making $y$ a ``compromise'' between $x$ and $z$, raises the relative probability of choosing $y$, compared to $x$.
The inequality on the right is similar, except that $x$ is added to $\{y,z\}$, again making $y$ a ``compromise'' between $x$ and $z$.
Our ``Beer'' domain includes three six-packs where a betweenness relation, relative to the price and quality rating attributes, applies.

The similarity and compromise effects are compatible with the conditions we consider in this paper, but not with others that may be of interest, such as the constant ratio rule described by \citeasnoun{Tver72b}.
See \citeasnoun{StobMarlMcCaTurn23} for details; different versions of context effects may differ in terms of their compatibility with different conditions.

\section{Models}\label{s:models}

In this section we describe models for stochastic discrete choice data.
These models treat choice objects as {\em a priori} indistinguishable, and we will use them to test conditions on random choice structures where objects are abstract.
A {\em choice dataset} for a universe $\univ$ consists of choices observed, over a number of trials, from various \menus{} included in $\univ$.
We denote a full dataset by $\mathbf{X}$ when it is interpreted as a random variable, and by $\mathbf{X}$ when interpreted as a possible fixed value.
A particular choice of a RCS $P \in \Delta$, together with the iid assumption, fully describes a data generating process for $\mathbf{X}$, the distribution $\mathbf{x}|P$.

A {\em Bayesian discrete choice model} consists of a prior distribution over the set $\Delta$ of discrete choice models.
Since the prior is a mixing distribution over choice probabilities, the mixture distribution also fully describes a data generating process for the choice dataset $\mathbf{x}$.
For example, if we have a Bayesian discrete choice model $M$ with prior density $f_M(P)$ on $\Delta$, the mixture distribution is the marginal probability mass function $f_M(\mathbf{x}) = \int_\Delta f(\mathbf{x}|P) f_M(P) \,dP$.
Evaluation of $f_M(\mathbf{x})$ at the observed value $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ gives the {\em marginal likelihood} $f_M(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}})$ of the data, a number that measures the predictive performance of $M$ for the observed $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}$.
Since it makes no reference to any unknown quantities, such as $P$, it can be interpreted as the out-of-sample prediction record of $M$ for the observed data $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}$.

To test conditions, we will compare {\em encompassing} models, Bayesian discrete choice models where the prior density $f(P)$ has full support on $\Delta$, with {\em constrained} models, where the condition of interest limits the support of $f(P)$ to the region $\Lambda \subseteq \Delta$ where the condition holds.
We have already seen examples $\Lambda_{\mathrm{Reg}}$, $\Lambda_{\mathrm{RU}}$ and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{MI}}$ of these regions.
A constrained model is a Bayesian discrete model whose prior density over $\Delta$ is the truncation of the prior density in an encompassing model, where the truncation is to the region $\Lambda$.
We will test conditions by computing Bayes factors---explained below---comparing constrained with encompassing models.
We will provide prior sensitivity analysis by comparing these Bayes factors across a set of encompassing models.
For example, we will perform three tests (indexed by $i \in \{1,2,3\}$) of  regularity.
Each test $i$ involves the comparison, based on Bayes factors, of the encompassing model $M_i$, a Bayesian discrete choice model whose prior density is $f_{M_i}(P)$, with a constrained Bayesian discrete choice model whose prior density is the trunction of $f_{M_i}(P)$ to $\Lambda_{\mathrm{Reg}}$.
The prior densities $f_{M_i}(P)$ of the encomposing models all have support $\Delta$ and the prior densities of the constrained models all have support $\Lambda_{\mathrm{Reg}}$.

Other studies have tested conditions by computing Bayes factors in favour of a constrained model against an encompassing model, including \citeasnoun{CavaDavi14}, \citeasnoun{DaviBrowCava15}, \citeasnoun{MyunKaraIver05} and \citeasnoun{ZwilCavaRege11}.
These used simple non-hierarchical priors on $P$ where the $P_A(\cdot)$ are {\em a priori} stochastically independent and uniformly distributed.
The stochastic independence across \menus{} means, for example, a Bayesian learner with such a prior learns nothing about how likely a decision maker is to choose $a$ from $\{a,c\}$ by observing choices from $\{a,b\}$ and $\{b,c\}$, no matter how decisively $a$ is chosen from $\{a,b\}$ and $b$ from $\{b,c\}$.

These studies analysed data featuring binary choice (or ternary choice where indifference between two objects is an option available to participants) and tested binary choice conditions such as different forms of stochastic transitivity and the triangle inequality.
Prior distributions where the $P_A(\cdot)$ are {\em a priori} stochastically independent are feasible for testing binary choice conditions because the implied prior probabilities of the conditions are high enough to measure using straightforward Monte Carlo simulation.
Conditions that constrain all choice probabilities, not just the binaries, such as regularity, the multiplicative inequality and the Block-Marschak inequalities, tend to have much lower prior probability.
For such conditions, it is important to introduce some stochastic dependence among the various choice probability vectors $P_A(\cdot)$.
The stochastic dependence also allows for Bayesian learning across \menus{}, even for the encompassing models.

For the encompassing models, we use a prior density for $P$ described in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl13}, defined up to the values of two parameters, $\alpha \in (0,\infty)$ and $\lambda \in [0,1]$.
We will denote this prior density as $f(P|\alpha,\lambda)$.
The prior has some attractive properties, described there, including invariance to permutations of object labels, which is desirable for inference whenever objects are treated as indistinguishable before seeing the data.

The parameter $\alpha$ specifies the marginal distribution over the various $P_A(\cdot)$ and describes how consistent choices are likely to be across trials.
For each \menu{} $A = \{x_1,\ldots,x_{|A|}\}$, the marginal distribution of the vector $(P_A(x_1), P_A(x_2), \ldots, P_A(x_{|A|}))$ is symmetric Dirichlet with all shape parameters set to $\alpha / |A|$.
Take for example, the \menus{} $\{a,b,c\}$ and $\{a,b\}$.
The distribution of choice probabilities on $\{a,b,c\}$ is $(P_{\{a,b,c\}}(a), P_{\{a,b,c\}}(a), P_{\{a,b,c\}}(a)) \sim \mathrm{Di}(\alpha/3, \alpha/3, \alpha/3)$, where $\mathrm{Di}$ denotes the Dirichlet distribution.
The distribution of choice probabilities on $\{a,b\}$ is $(P_{\{a,b\}}(a), P_{\{a,b\}}(b)) \sim \mathrm{Di}(\alpha/2, \alpha/2)$, or in other terms $P_{\{a,b\}}(a) \sim \mathrm{Be}(\alpha/2,\alpha/2)$, the Beta distribution with both shape parameters set to $\alpha/2$.
Figure \ref{f:bcp} shows the $\Be(\alpha/2,\alpha/2)$ densities of binary choice probabilities, for six different values of $\alpha$.

Low values of $\alpha$ are associated with more consistent, or less variable, choice.
For $\alpha < |A|$, the density of $P$ goes to infinity as any one choice probability goes to one and choice becomes deterministic; for $A = \{a,b,c\}$, this happens as $P$ approaches $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ or $(0,0,1)$.
We see in Figure \ref{f:bcp}, for example, that for $\alpha = 1$, the density of any binary choice probability approaches infinity as the probability goes to zero or one.
As $\alpha$ gets closer to zero, more probability is concentrated in regions where probabilities are close to zero or one, and choice becomes more determinstic.

High values of $\alpha$ are associated with more variable choice.
For $\alpha > |A|$, $(P_A(a_1), P_A(a_2), \ldots, P_A(a_{|A|}))$ has a mode at $(1/|A|, \ldots, 1/|A|)$, which is the uniform discrete (choice) distribution over the elements of $A$, and the density approaches zero as any probability goes to zero.
As $\alpha$ gets larger, more probability is concentrated near this uniform-discrete-choice mode and choice becomes more variable and unpredictable.
Figure \ref{f:bcp} shows that as $\alpha$ increases from 5 to 10 to 20, the mode (and mean) of any binary choice probability stays at $\tfrac{1}{2}$ but its variance decreases.
For the intermediate case $\alpha = |A|$, $P_A(\cdot)$ is uniformly distributed on the probability simplex, as we see in Figure \ref{f:bcp} for $\alpha=2$.

The parameter $\lambda \in [0,1]$ describes the degree of dependence of choice probabilities across \menus{}.
The higher the value, the more a Bayesian learner updates across \menus{} and the higher the prior probability that RU holds.
For $\lambda = 0$, the choice probability vectors $P_A(\cdot)$ are mutually independent, across $A \subseteq T$.
As $\lambda$ increases, the dependance of the $P_A(\cdot)$ across \menu{}s resembles more the dependance associated with random utility.
For $\lambda = 1$, the random choice structure satisfies random utility with probability one.
For $\lambda = 1$, and only this value, the prior does not have full support on $\Delta$.
Details on the prior distributions for $P$, including some of its desirable properties, are given in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl13}.

It remains to specify the unknown parameters $\alpha$ and $\lambda$.
It is possible to choose fixed values, but a more flexible approach is to provide a prior distribution for $\alpha$ and $\lambda$, which allows for learning about plausible values of $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ through data.
The result is an example of a hierarchical prior.
Just as $P$ can be marginalized out to obtain the marginal density $f_M(\mathbf{x})$ when using a non-hierarchical prior density $f_M(P)$ for $P$, we can marginalize out $\alpha$, $\lambda$ and $P$ using a hierarchical prior consisting of a prior density $f_M(\alpha,\lambda)$ for $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ and the density $f(P|\alpha,\lambda)$ in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl13}.
The result is the marginal density
\[
  f_M(\mathbf{x}) = \int f(\mathbf{x}|P) f(P|\alpha,\lambda) f_M(\alpha,\lambda) \, dP\, d\alpha\, d\lambda.
\]
The marginal likelihood $f_M(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}})$ is the value of this marginal density at the observed choice dataset $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}$.

Following \citeasnoun{McCaMarl14} and \citeasnoun{McCaStobMarlParkBrow20}, we specify an encompassing model using a hierarchical prior distribution for $P$ consisting of the prior density $f(P|\alpha,\lambda)$ and a prior distribution of $(\alpha,\lambda)$ where $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ are {\em a priori} independent, $\alpha$ has a Gamma distribution with shape parameter $a_1 + a_2$ and rate parameter $b$, $\lambda$ has a Beta distribution with shape parameters $a_1$ and $a_2$.
As in those papers, we perform a prior sensitivity analysis, using various choices of the hyper-parameter vector $(a_1,a_2,b)$, to illustrate the sensitivity of results to the choice of prior in the encompassing model.
The particular choices of $(a_1,a_2,b)$ we use here are different from those used in these previous studies.
In part, this is because the present paper is concerned with population, rather than individual choice.
Anticipating some heterogeneity across individuals, we shift prior weight towards higher values of $\alpha$, and therefore away from choice probabilities close to zero or one.
Also, we learned from the analysis of these previous experiments that results were fairly insensitive to the prior for $\alpha$, and that values of $\lambda$ close to one were strongly favoured.
Accordingly, we use the same prior for $\alpha$ for each encompassing model and put more prior weight on values of $\lambda$ closer to one than in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl14} and \citeasnoun{McCaStobMarlParkBrow20}.

Table \ref{t:hyper} shows the values of $(a_1, a_2, b)$ for three encompassing models, $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$, used in the empirical analysis.
In Section \ref{s:results}, we will report results for these three models, illustrating the sensitivity of results to the choice of prior distribution.
The encompassing models feature dependence of $P_A(\cdot)$ across $A$, which makes them feasible for testing the conditions like Reg, MI and RU that constrain choice probabilities over all \menus{}.
The table also indicates the prior mean, variance and standard deviation of $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ for each encompassing model.
As we go from model $M_1$ to $M_2$ and then $M_3$, the prior distribution of $\lambda$ concentrates more and more probability mass near $\lambda = 1$, where RU is satisfied with probability one.

\section{Inferential Methods}\label{s:inference}

For any given choice dataset, our principal inferential objective is to test various discrete choice conditions, using Bayes factors comparing models that are constrained by these condition, to encompassing models, that are unconstrained.
A secondary inferential objective, not pursued extensively here, is to describe the posterior distribution of $P$ and the parameters $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ of the encompassing or the constrained models.
Both inferential exercises are based on posterior simulation methods outlined in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl14}.
For an overview of Bayesian theory, see \citeasnoun{BernSmit94} or \citeasnoun{Berg85}.
For more on Bayes factors in particular and their interpretation, see \citeasnoun{KassRaft95}.

The posterior distribution $\alpha,\lambda,P|\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}$, represents what we learn, relative to the prior distribution of $\alpha$, $\lambda$ and $P$, from the choice dataset $\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}$.
The posterior distribution is analytically intractable and we use Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to obtain a large posterior sample targeting the posterior distribution.
The posterior sample does not consist of independent draws, but there are standard methods for quantifying simulation error; that is, the difference between posterior sample moments and their population counterparts.
We use the overlapping batch means (OBM) method, whose properties are described in \citeasnoun{FlegJone10}, to compute the numerical standard error of posterior sample means.

We numerically compute Bayes factors for all model comparisons.
Suppose we are comparing any two Bayesian discrete choice models $M$ and $M'$, and we observe a choice dataset $\mathbf{x}$.
Then the posterior odds ratio giving the relative posterior probabilities of $M$ and $M'$ can be expressed as
\[
  \frac{\Pr[M|\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}]}{\Pr[M'|\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}]} = \frac{\Pr[M]}{\Pr[M']}
  \cdot
  \frac{f_{M}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt})}{f_{M'}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt})}
  %\frac{\Pr[\mathbf{x}|M]}{\Pr[\mathbf{x}|M']}
\]
This result follows easily from Bayes' rule and does not depend on how many models are under consideration.
The expression on the right hand side is the product of two factors.
The first is the prior odds ratio and the second is a ratio of marginal likelihoods called the {\em Bayes factor}---see \citeasnoun{Berg85} or \citeasnoun{BernSmit94} for further explanation.
The marginal likelihoods $f_{M}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt})$ and $f_{M'}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt})$ are the probabilities of observing the choice data $\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}$ if the models $M$ and $M'$ (respectively) are true.
The ratio measures the relative out-of-sample predictive performance of the two models, with a value greater t{}han one favouring model $M$.

In the special case where the denominator model is an encompassing model $M_e$ and the numerator model is the constrained model $M_c$ obtained by truncating the density $f_{M_e}(P)$ associated with $M_e$ to the region $\Lambda$ defining a condition, then the Bayes factor becomes
\begin{equation}\label{e:BF}
  \frac{f_{M_c}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt})}{f_{M_e}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt})} =
  \frac{\Pr_{M_e}[\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}|P \in \Lambda]}{\Pr_{M_e}[\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}]} = \frac{\Pr_{M_e}[P \in \Lambda|\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_\mathrm{opt}]}{\Pr_{M_e}[P \in \Lambda]}.
\end{equation}

The second equation of \eqref{e:BF} follows from Bayes' rule; see \citeasnoun{KlugHoij07} for details; the far right hand side of \eqref{e:BF} is the ratio of posterior to prior probability of the condition holding according to the encompassing model.{}
The larger the numerator, the more probable the condition is in light of the data; the smaller the denominator, the more restrictive the condition is.
This ratio also suggests a practical way of computing approximations of Bayes factors: since the probability of an event is just the expectation of an indicator function for the event, we can approximate numerator and denominator using Monte Carlo methods.

We compute prior and posterior probabilities of conditions numerically using the simulation methods described in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl14}.
We compute numerical standard errors of prior and posterior probabilities using the OBM method and combine these standard errors to compute standard errors for Bayes factors using the delta method, introduced in \citeasnoun{Kell28}.

\section{Results}\label{s:results}

We begin by discussing constraints on generality.
We then show some data summaries for one of the experimental domains, for illustrative purposes.
The document \texttt{all\_domains\_data.pdf} in the folder ``Supplementary materials'' of the Github repository \texttt{https://github.com/mccauslw/Population} provides the same data summary for the other domains.

\subsection{Constraints on generality}

We would expect choice proportions for the domains studied here to vary across populations and through time.
We suspect, for example, that other populations may be more or less knowledegable about the latitude of North American and European cities and that tastes in actors evolve over time.
We conjecture that the patterns of choice probabilities that we find here, in terms of their adherence to various conditions on discrete choice, are more universal, but this is ultimately an empirical question.

\subsection{Tests of context effects using Bayes factors}

Here we provide a worked example showing how to apply Bayesian model comparison methods, supported by Monte Carlo simulation tools, to test whether or not a context effect holds in a given situation.
We take the Latitude domain and consider six possible attraction effects, based on the dominance relation, proposed in Section \ref{s:intro}, that puts Vancouver over Seattle and London over Paris.
Each row of Table \ref{t:colours_table} describes one candidate attraction effect and reports test results for the hypothesis that the effect holds.
The first three columns identify the target (T), competitor (C) and decoy (D) options that define the case.
The fourth column gives the number of participants that choose the target and competitor, respectively, when the menu is this pair of options.
The fifth column gives the number of participants that choose the target, competitor and decoy, respectively, when the menu is this three-element set.

For the encompassing model, we choose the simple prior distribution where the two probability vectors $(P_{\{T,C\}}(T), P_{\{T,C\}}(C))$ and $(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T), P_{\{T,C,D\}}(C), P_{\{T,C,D\}}(D))$ are independent and each is uniformly distributed on the region where its elements are non-negative and add to one.
Thus, the prior distribution of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1,1)$ and that of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1,2)$, where $\mathrm{Be}(\alpha,\beta)$ denotes the Beta distribution with shape parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

The prior independence of the choice probabilities $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ and $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ and the independence of participants choices, within and across menus, imply the posterior independence of these probabilities.
The posterior distribution of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(T), 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(C))$ and that of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ is $\mathrm{Be}(1 + N_{\{T,C,D\}}(T), 2 + N_{\{T,C,D\}}(C)) + N_{\{T,C,D\}}(D))$. 

The hypothesis we wish to test is that the attraction effect holds, or equivalently, that $P_{\{T,C\}}(T) < P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
Taking $\Lambda$ to be the event that $P_{\{T,C\}}(T) < P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, we truncate the prior distribution for the encompassing model to the set $\Lambda$ and use this as the prior distribution for the constrained model.
Then the Bayes factor in favour of the constrained model over the encompassing model is $\Pr[\Lambda|N]/\Pr[\Lambda]$, the ratio of posterior to prior probability of the effect under the encompassing model.

We use Monte Carlo simulation methods to estimate the numerator $\Pr[\Lambda]$ and denominator $\Pr[\Lambda|N]$ of the Bayes factor.
We will apply a variance reduction technique called Rao-Blackwellization to obtain high numerical precision.
Denote the cumulative distribution function for the distribution $\mathrm{Be}(\alpha, \beta)$ by $F_{\mathrm{Be}}(\cdot;\alpha, \beta)$.
Then $F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1, 1)$, the prior cdf of $P_{\{T,C\}}(T)$ evaluated at the random variable $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, is the conditional prior probability of $\Lambda$ given $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
Therefore $E[F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1, 1)]$ is the marginal prior probability $\Pr[\Lambda]$, where the expectation is taken with respect to the prior distribution of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$.
Similarly, $F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(T), 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(C))$ is the conditional {\em posterior} probability of $\Lambda$ given $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$ and its posterior expectation is $\Pr[\Lambda|N]$.

As estimators of these expectations, we use their sample mean counterparts.
To estimate $\Pr[\Lambda]$, we first draw a iid sample of size $M$ from the {\em prior} distribution of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$: $P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, $m=1,\ldots,M$.
The standard strong law of large numbers for independent draws and bounded random variables ensures the following almost sure (a.s.) convergence:
\[
	\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1, 1)
	\overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \Pr[\Lambda],
\]
Similarly, for an iid sample $P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, $m=1,\ldots,M$, from the {\em posterior} distribution of $P_{\{T,C,D\}}(T)$, the distribution $\mathrm{Be}(1 + N_{\{T,C,D\}}, 2 + N_{\{T,C,D\}}(C) + N_{\{T,C,D\}}(C))$, we have 
\[
	\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M F_{\mathrm{Be}}(P^{(m)}_{\{T,C,D\}}(T); 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(T), 1 + N_{\{T,C\}}(C))
	\overset{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \Pr[\Lambda|N].
\]
We estimated $\Pr[\Lambda]$ and $\Pr[\Lambda|N]$ using samples of size $M = 10^6$.
We computed the usual numerical standard errors of the estimators and found all to be less than 0.0003.

Columns six and seven of Table \ref{t:asy_dom_table} give numerical estimates of $\Pr[\Lambda]$ and $\Pr[\Lambda|N]$, the prior and posterior probabilities of the attraction effect under the encompassing model.
The final two columns give the Bayes Factor (BF), equal to the ratio $\Pr[\Lambda|N]/\Pr[\Lambda]$, and its natural logarithm.

The evidence favours the attraction effect for the three cases where the target is Vancouver and the decoy is Seattle.
In constrast, it favours the encompassing model for the three cases where the target is London and the decoy is Paris.
Observations from menus consisting of the proposed target and decoy options provide some independent evidence on the plausibility of the two dominance relations, and these suggest that Seattle is a more convincing decoy for Vancouver than Paris is for London.
Of the participants who saw the menu of Vancouver and Seattle, 36 chose Vancouver and 4 chose Seattle.
Of those who saw the menu of London and Paris, only 32 chose London and 8 chose Paris.

We can use the guidelines recommended by \citeasnoun{KassRaft95} to classify the strength of the evidence provided by the Bayes Factors.
They classify log Bayes factors between 0 and 1 as ``not worth more than a bare mention'', those between 1 and 3 as providing ``positive evidence'', those between 3 and 5 as providing ``strong evidence'' and those above 5 as providing ``very strong evidence''.
We will adopt these categories throughout the paper, but refer to the first two as giving ``weak'' evidence and ``moderate'' evidence.

By this reckoning, there is moderate evidence in favour of the attraction effect when the target is Vancouver, the competitor is Paris and the decoy is Seattle and moderate evidence against the attraction effect when the target is London, the decoy is Paris and the competitor is either Vancouver or Seattle.

We can also consider joint hypotheses, such as the hypothesis that the attraction effect holds in all three cases where the target is Vancouver and the decoy is Seattle.
The log Bayes factor in favour of this joint hypothesis is the sum of the individual log Bayes factors, or 2.253.
This is stronger evidence, but still not ``strong'' according to the standards of \citeasnoun{KassRaft95}.
The log Bayes factor in favour of the joint hypothesis that the attraction effect holds in all three cases where the target is London and the decoy is Paris is -3.744, which implies ``strong'' evidence against the joint hypothesis.

\subsection{Tests of abstract conditions using Bayes factors}

For each choice domain, we computed log\footnote{All logarithms are natural (base $e$) logarithms.} marginal likelihoods for the three encompassing models, $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$, and for the constrained models defined by all possible pairs of encompassing model and condition (Reg, RU or MI).
Recall that the log marginal likelihood summarizes the out-of-sample prediction record of a model, and the log Bayes factor for comparing any two of these twelve models is the difference between their log marginal likelihoods.

We report log Bayes factors for all twelve models relative to the encompassing model $M_3$, to establish a reference level of zero on a scale of out-of-sample predictive performance; by definition, the log Bayes factor for $M_3$ relative to itself is zero.
We choose $M_3$ as the reference model because it had the best out-of-sample prediction record (measured by log marginal likelihood) of the three encompassing models for 31 out of the 32 domains, but the choice is fairly arbitrary.
All other Bayes factors can be computed as differences of these: a Bayes factor is the difference between the marginal likelihoods of two models, also equal to the difference between their respective Bayes factors relative to the {\em same} reference model, here $M_3$.

Figure \ref{f:multiple_BF} gives, for each choice domain, log Bayes factors for various combinations of condition and encompassing model, relative to encompassing model $M_3$.
Bayes factors between any two models can be read off as vertical distances between two points.
There is a panel for each domain, and each panel shows three columns of points, corresponding to the three encompassing models $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$.
Each column $i=1,2,3$ shows the Bayes factors for all models based on the encompassing model $M_i$: the circle gives the Bayes factor for the (unconstrained) encompassing model $M_i$ itself, relative to $M_3$.
The Bayes factor for $M_3$, relative to itself is zero by definition and so the solid dot in the third column always indicates the value zero.
In each column $i=1,2,3$, the triangle, square and cross give log Bayes factors of the constrained model consisting of the restriction of $M_i$ to the regions $\Lambda_{\mathrm{Reg}}$, $\Lambda_{\mathrm{RU}}$ and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{MI}}$, respectively, all relative to the reference model $M_3$.
Thus, for example, the vertical distance between the triangle and the dot in column $i$ gives the Bayes factor in favour of regularity, for the encompassing model $M_i$.

Recall that variation in results among the three encompassing models is used for prior sensitivity analysis.
Comparisons of Bayes factors across columns illustrate how results depend on changes to the prior specification.

Note that there are no crosses for the choice domains ``Latitude'', ``Itineraries I'' and ``Payoffs'', whatever the encompassing model.
This is because the posterior probability of the multiplicative inequality is very close to zero.
This makes it very difficult to measure the log Bayes factor precisely, but we can conclude that it is very large, implying a strong rejection of that condition for those domains.
Evidence in support of the multiplicative inequality varies a lot across choice domain, from moderate evidence in favour to very strong evidence against.
The evidence is fairly robust to the choice of encompassing model.

For regularity and random utility, the evidence for a given domain tends to be weaker, but more consistent across choice domains.
In most cases, the log Bayes factors either favour random utility and regularity over the encompassing models, or are very close to zero, representing little evidence either way.
Across domains and encompassing models, the evidence in favour of random utility is very similar to the evidence in favour of regularity, which is a necessary condition for random utility.
In most cases, the evidence in favour of regularity is greater than that in favour of random utility, indicating some evidence against the higher order features of random utility, but this evidence is quite weak.
For individual domains, the evidence in favour of random utility and regularity are at best slight, but collectively, the evidence is much stronger: the log Bayes factor in favour of regularity holding jointly across domains is the sum over domains of the log Bayes factors in favour of regularity, and likewise for random utility.
We see that the variation in predictive performance of random utility across encompassing models (height of the square, across columns, for a particular domain) is much less than the variation in predictive performance across the encompassing models themselves (height of the circle, across columns, for the same domain).
This is consistent with a kind of model mimicry, whereby imposing random utility, on one hand, and increasing the prior dependence of $P_A(\cdot)$ across \menus{} $A$, as measured by $\lambda$, on the other hand, have similar implications for observables.
The same observation applies to regularity.

\subsection{Posterior distributions of $\alpha$ and $\lambda$}

Recall that the concentration parameter $\alpha$ governs how consistent choices are likely to be from trial to trial.
As $\alpha$ approaches zero, choice probabilities near zero or one become more probable, and choice becomes more consistent from trial to trial; as $\alpha$ approaches infinity, choice probabilities near the discrete uniform (where objects in a menu are equally likely to be chosen) become more probable and choice becomes more variable across trials.
We would expect $\alpha$ to vary considerably across domains.
Not surprisingly, the posterior distribution of $\alpha$ varies from domain to domain.
The domain with the least posterior median was ``Surface area'', where the posterior median was 5.6 and the $90\%$ credible set\footnote{computed as the interval from the 5th percentile to the 95th} was $[3.1,9.4]$.
The domain with the greatest posterior median was ``Colour Combos'', with a posterior median of 17.4 and a credible set of $[9.1,30.5]$.

Values of the posterior median of $\alpha$ are much higher than those reported in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl14} and \citeasnoun{McCaStobMarlParkBrow20}, which analysed data from experiments with a within-subject design.
Again, this is not surprising.
We would expect the repeated choices of a single individual to be more consistent than the choices of a sample of individuals.

The $\lambda \in [0,1]$ parameter is of more theoretical interest because it governs the degree of dependence of choice probabilities across \menus{}.
At one extreme, $\lambda = 0$, they are mutually independent, and at the other, $\lambda = 1$, the random choice structure satisfies random utility with probability one.
The posterior quantiles of $\lambda$ for $p=0.75$ and $p=0.95$ are very close to one for all domains and so the details are not very informative.
This concentration of posterior probability very near $\lambda = 1$ is similar to what \citeasnoun{McCaStobMarlParkBrow20} found for a large majority of the 140 participants in the within-subject experiment described there.
However, while there were a few exceptions in that previous experiment, there are no domains in the present experiment that are exceptional in this regard.
This gives strong evidence for a kind of posterior dependence of the $P_A(\cdot)$ across \menus{} $A$ similar to that induced by the random utility condition.
In some sense, putting higher prior probability mass near $\lambda = 1$ on the one hand, and imposing random utility on the other, have similar implications for observables: Figure \ref{f:multiple_BF} above shows that for every domain, the Bayes factors in favour of random utility (the three black squares in each panel) vary much less across the three underlying encompassing models than the Bayes factors in favour of the encompassing models themselves (the black circles).

\section{Conclusions}\label{s:conclude}

We have collected new experimental data for population choice probabilities.
Several features of the experimental design make the data suitable for testing conditions on discrete probabilistic choice at the population level.

First, it includes a wide variety of choice domains.
Some choice domains involve subjective aesthetic judgements, on such matters as food and drink, art, music, politics and the grammaticality of sentences.
Other choice domains resemble choice domains that naturally occur in markets, such as those for mobile phone plans, hotel rooms, beer, cars and flights.
Still others involve options having an objective rank order.
Some choice domains have options that are distinguished only by the levels of numerical attributes, others do not.
Some domains are designed to elicit context effects, others are not.
This variety helps us and others to discover properties of random choice structures that hold widely.
An unintended but welcome additional consequence was the high level of engagement on the part of participants.
Evidence for this comes from written feedback left by participants, much of which indicated a high level of interest in the experiment.
A complete compilation of feedback from participants is available in the document \texttt{feedback.pdf} in the folder ``Supplementary materials'' of the Github repository \texttt{https://github.com/mccauslw/Population}.

%The median participant took $18.75s$ per domain; three quarters of them took longer than $13.31s$ per domain.
%The domain ``Latitude'', where participants are prompted with the question ``Which one of the following cities do you think is furthest north'' is one of the domains whose elements have an objective order.
%In this domain, there are two pairs of cities where the objective order of latitude should be familiar to most people: one is ``London, United Kingdom'' and ``Paris, France''; the other is ``Vancouver, Canada'' and ``Seattle, United States''.
%We would expect most attentive participants to choose the city that is actually furthest north.
%Indeed, of the 40 participants offered the binary choice between London and Paris, 32 chose London and of the 40 participants offered the binary choice between Vancouver and Seattle, 36 chose Vancouver.
%Recall that the participants are residents of Canada.

Second, for each choice domain, we observed repeated choices from all 26 doubleton and larger subsets of a universe of five objects, allowing us to test any condition on discrete probabilistic choice by exposing every implication of it to possible falsification.
This is particularly important for testing conditions like Reg, RU and MI, for which the choice probability vector for every menu is pivotal, in the sense defined in Section \ref{s:axioms}.

Third, by arranging that every participant saw exactly one \menu{} from each choice domain, we obtain data on population choices that are plausibly independent as well as identically distributed, \menu{} by \menu{}.

The experimental data we have collected complement the models introduced in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl13} and the inferential methods described in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl14}.
Together, the models, methods and data serve as a testing ground for the conditions we have considered in this paper and any others that one may wish to investigate.
That said, the data are publicly available and others can use different statistical methods for analyzing them.

Evidence in support of regularity and random utility is fairly consistent across choice domains.
Both conditions correspond to convex regions ($\Lambda_{Reg}$ and $\Lambda_{RU}$) of the space $\Delta$ of RCSs, and therefore aggregate across individuals.
While the evidence in favour of these conditions for any one choice domain is not strong, the collective evidence is quite favourable.
It is important to note too, that the encompassing models used for computing Bayes factors already feature a great deal of prior dependence among choice probability vectors $P_A(\cdot)$ (as measured by $\lambda$) similar to the kind of dependence associated with random utility, and that encompassing models with a higher prior mean of $\lambda$ are favoured.
While the literature on the assymmetric dominance effect provides evidence against regularity and therefore random utility in special cases, these conditions hold up across the wide variety of choice domains we explore, including a few domains designed to elicit asymmetric dominance effects.
This is reassuring given the prevalence of random utility models in the wide literature on discrete choice.

Evidence regarding the multiplicative inequality, in contrast, varies considerably across domains.
There is very strong evidence against it for three domains, so we can conclude that it is not a promising candidate for a universal condition governing probabilistic discrete choice.
\citeasnoun{Tver72a} introduced the EBA class of models to accommodate the similarity effect introduced in the same paper, but since the multiplicative inequality is a necessary condition for EBA models, there are clearly other observed choice patterns that it cannot capture well.

However, the multiplicative inequality is favoured more than random utility in many domains, which raises the question of what common features these domains might have.
In future work we wish to investigate the multiplicative inequality in more detail, using a variety of data sources, complementing some theoretical work on that condition in \citeasnoun{McCaMarl24}.
Using the data from the experiment described in the present paper, we hope to explore the occurrence (or not) of context effects in those domains that are designed to elicit them, and also to see if similar patterns of probabilities occur in domains where choice objects do not have obvious numerical attributes.

In ongoing work, McCausland (unpublished) computes marginal likelihoods for random preference models corresponding to $\lambda = 1$ in the model of \citeasnoun{McCaMarl13}.
This will allow for cleaner tests of random preference and random utility models, against a wider set of alternative models.

We classify choice domains into four categories.

We adopt the convention that whenever there is a natural order for the items in a list, we order them in decreasing order.
Again, this is only for the purposes of reporting results; each participant sees the items in a \menu{} in random order.
